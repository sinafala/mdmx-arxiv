3.1 What the files look like

Each HMD bulk file holds every population in one whitespace-separated table. The first line names the series and gives its “Last modified” stamp, the second is blank, and the third is the column header. Missing values are written as a single dot, and the oldest age group is written 110+ (the open interval: everyone aged 110 or more).

3.2 Reading the files

mdmx.data.ingest_life_tables() reads the female and male files with pandas (splitting on runs of whitespace and treating . as missing), converts 110+ to the integer 110, and stacks the two sexes into one long table with one row per population × sex × year × age. A long table is the natural format for a data pipeline: every filter, join and summary is a one-liner, and each row is one fact. ingest_deaths_exposures() does the same for the death counts and exposures, which are published with female, male and total columns side by side and are reshaped to the same long form.

Both tables are saved as artefacts — Parquet files in work/companion/artifacts/ with a provenance record that names their input (the verified release) — so later chapters load them in a fraction of a second instead of re-parsing 200 MB of text.

3.3.3 The age pattern of mortality

The probability of dying, \(q_x\), has the familiar U (or J) shape: high in infancy, lowest around age 10, then rising roughly exponentially with age. The logit transform, \(\operatorname{logit}(q) = \log\{q/(1-q)\}\), maps probabilities onto the whole real line; on that scale the old-age rise is close to linear, variability is more even across ages, and differences between schedules are additive — which is why the whole analysis works with \(\operatorname{logit}(q_x)\) (paper §2.2, “The quantity modelled”).

Schedules used to illustrate the age pattern 
# Russia's HMD series ends in 2014, so its most recent table is used.
SELECTION = [("SWE", 1800), ("SWE", 1900), ("SWE", 2020), ("JPN", 2020),
             ("USA", 2020), ("RUS", 2014)]
def schedule(pop, year, sex):
    s = lt48[(lt48["pop"] == pop) & (lt48["year"] == year) & (lt48["sex"] == sex)
             & (lt48["age"] <= P.data.age_max)].sort_values("age")
    return s["age"].to_numpy(), s["qx"].to_numpy()

3.3.4 The sex differential

On the logit scale the male–female difference \(\operatorname{logit}(q^{m}_x) - \operatorname{logit}(q^{f}_x)\) is a log odds ratio. Positive values mean higher male mortality, which is the case at almost every age; the excess is largest in young adulthood and has grown since the nineteenth century.

Code 
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.5))
years = [1800, 1850, 1900, 1950, 2000, 2020]
for year, colour in zip(years, plt.cm.viridis(np.linspace(0, 0.9, len(years)))):

The probability of dying, $q_x$, has the familiar U (or J) shape: high in
infancy, lowest around age 10, then rising roughly exponentially with age.
The logit transform, $\operatorname{logit}(q) = \log\{q/(1-q)\}$, maps
