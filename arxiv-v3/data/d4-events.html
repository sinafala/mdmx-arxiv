5.1 Why exceptional years are set aside

Wars and pandemics produce short, sharp mortality shocks with their own age–sex signatures. The Tucker decomposition is meant to describe the baseline mortality surface — the slow secular transition — so the years in which a population was hit by such an event are removed from the data the decomposition sees, and kept aside for the disruption model of paper §6 (chapter 08). A country-year is exceptional if, and only if, it appears in the event dictionary.

5.2 The event dictionary

The dictionary is a plain table, config/events.csv, with one row per event and group of populations: the event, its type, first and last calendar year, the HMD population codes affected, and a source. ALL means every population with data during the event (used for the influenza pandemics of 1957–58 and 1968–69 and for COVID-19). Keeping the dictionary as data, rather than as code, means the same file drives both the masks used in the analysis and the dictionary’s tables at the end of this chapter, so the two can never disagree.

5.2.1 How the dictionary is compiled

Four choices shape the dictionary; where an entry needs a reason, the note column above gives it.

  • The United Kingdom (GBR_NP) is listed with its parts. It is a population in its own right (chapter d1), so every war entry that lists England & Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland lists it too. Otherwise its war years, 1939–45 for example, would count as normal years while those of its parts are masked.
  • Entry dates. Each belligerent is flagged from the year it entered the war, so that its years before entry stay in the baseline: Italy and Bulgaria in World War I from 1915, the United States and Greece from 1917; Italy and Greece in World War II from 1940 (the same entry lists Hungary and Bulgaria, which entered in 1941; their HMD series begin after the war), the United States from 1942; France and Britain in the Crimean War from 1854 and Sardinia (Italy) from 1855. Two entries start earlier for some of the populations they list: World War II from 1939 for every population of its main entry, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark and Norway (invaded in 1940) included, and the Napoleonic Wars from 1803 for Sweden, at war from 1805. The populations that sent forces to the Second Boer War and the Korean War are flagged for those wars too, though little excess mortality is expected at home.
  • Sweden in the Seven Years’ War is flagged for 1757–1762, the years of its own (Pomeranian) war within the war of 1756–1763.
  • A fourth type, “other”, records four mortality crises that are neither wars nor the three pandemic types: the Swedish famine and dysentery epidemic of 1772–73 (the lowest life expectancy in the HMD), the Icelandic measles epidemics of 1846 and 1882, and the 1871 smallpox epidemic in the Netherlands and Belgium. These years are excluded from the baseline like other exceptional years, but they are too few and too heterogeneous to estimate a disruption profile, so the disruption model does not use them.

5.3 Labelling every country-year

Each curated country-year receives a label \(d_{c,t}\): 0 = none, 1 = war, 2 = respiratory pandemic, 3 = enteric pandemic, 4 = other. When a country-year is in more than one entry the label is chosen by priority war > respiratory > enteric > other (paper §2.3): in 1918, for example, a belligerent is labelled war. All matching entries are kept in the all_events column for transparency.

5.4 A screen for crises the dictionary might miss

The dictionary is compiled from historical sources, so it can miss events. As a check, the code below lists every unlabelled country-year whose life expectancy (average of the sexes) falls more than 5.0 years below the median of the same population’s surrounding ±5 years. This is a diagnostic only — nothing is changed automatically. The four “other” crises were identified this way and confirmed against historical sources. The country-years still listed below remain in the baseline: most are in the very small Icelandic population, where single-year life expectancy is volatile, and the rest are isolated dips without an established cause in the sources used for the dictionary. They are listed so that any reader can judge them.

List unlabelled years with unusually low e0 
e0avg = (lt_cur[lt_cur["age"] == 0].groupby(["pop", "year"], as_index=False)["ex"].mean()
         .rename(columns={"ex": "e0"}))

5.5 Appendix tables for the paper

Four tables list the dictionary, one per type; the article does not show them. They are generated from the same CSV file used above.

Write the appendix tables (one per type) 
TITLES = {"war": "War event dictionary", "respiratory": "Respiratory pandemic dictionary",
          "enteric": "Enteric pandemic dictionary", "other": "Other mortality crises"}
def _pop_list(cell):
    # A wrapped list breaks only between populations: the spaces inside a name are non-breaking.
    if cell.strip() == "ALL":
        return "All populations with data in these years"
    return ", ".join(n.replace(" ", "\u00a0") for n in dict.fromkeys(names.get(c, c) for c in cell.split()))
for t in TYPES:
    sub = events[events["type"] == t]
    tab = pd.DataFrame({
        "Event": sub["event"],
        "Years": [f"{a}" if a == b else f"{a}--{b}" for a, b in zip(sub["start"], sub["end"])],
        "HMD populations": [_pop_list(c) for c in sub["populations"]],
    })
    latex.save_table(tab, f"tbl-app-{t}", f"{TITLES[t]}.  Generated from \\texttt{{config/events.csv}}.",
                     column_format=(r">{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{1.6cm}"
                                    r">{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr\textwidth-6.2cm-4\tabcolsep\relax}"),
                     long=True)

```{python}
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(11, 6), sharey=True)
for ax, pop in zip(axes.flat, SHOW):
    for sex in data.SEXES:
        s = e0[(e0["pop"] == pop) & (e0["sex"] == sex)].sort_values("year")
        ax.plot(s["year"], s["ex"], color=plotting.COLORS[sex], lw=0.8)
        for lab, t in enumerate(TYPES, start=1):
            hit = s[s["label"] == lab]
            ax.scatter(hit["year"], hit["ex"], s=8, color=plotting.COLORS[t], zorder=3,
                       label=t.title() if (pop == SHOW[0] and sex == "female") else None)
    ax.set_title(names[pop], fontsize=10)
axes[0, 0].legend(fontsize=7)
for ax in axes[:, 0]:
    ax.set_ylabel(r"$e_0$")
plotting.save_figure(fig, "fig-s2-events-e0")
```

## A screen for crises the dictionary might miss

The dictionary is compiled from historical sources, so it can miss events.
As a check, the code below lists every *unlabelled* country-year whose
life expectancy (average of the sexes) falls more than
`{python} P.data.crisis_screen_drop` years below the median of the same population's
surrounding ±`{python} P.data.crisis_screen_halfwidth` years.  This is a **diagnostic
only** — nothing is changed automatically.  The four "other" crises were
identified this way and confirmed against historical sources.  The
country-years still listed below remain in the baseline: most are in the
very small Icelandic population, where single-year life expectancy is
volatile, and the rest are isolated dips without an established cause in
the sources used for the dictionary.
They are listed so that any reader can judge them.

```{python}
#| label: d4-screen
#| code-summary: "List unlabelled years with unusually low e0"
e0avg = (lt_cur[lt_cur["age"] == 0].groupby(["pop", "year"], as_index=False)["ex"].mean()
         .rename(columns={"ex": "e0"}))
screen = data.crisis_screen(e0avg, labels, drop=P.data.crisis_screen_drop,
                            halfwidth=P.data.crisis_screen_halfwidth)
artifacts.save("crisis_screen", screen, inputs=["lt_curated", "event_labels"],
               params=P.section("data"), desc="Unlabelled country-years with unusually low e0")
screen.assign(name=screen["pop"].map(names)).round(1)
```

## Appendix tables for the paper

Four tables list the dictionary, one per type; the article does not show them.  They are
generated from the same CSV file used above.

```{python}
#| label: d4-appendix
#| code-summary: "Write the appendix tables (one per type)"
TITLES = {"war": "War event dictionary", "respiratory": "Respiratory pandemic dictionary",
          "enteric": "Enteric pandemic dictionary", "other": "Other mortality crises"}
def _pop_list(cell):
    # A wrapped list breaks only between populations: the spaces inside a name are non-breaking.
    if cell.strip() == "ALL":
        return "All populations with data in these years"
    return ", ".join(n.replace(" ", "\u00a0") for n in dict.fromkeys(names.get(c, c) for c in cell.split()))
for t in TYPES:
    sub = events[events["type"] == t]
    tab = pd.DataFrame({
        "Event": sub["event"],
        "Years": [f"{a}" if a == b else f"{a}--{b}" for a, b in zip(sub["start"], sub["end"])],
        "HMD populations": [_pop_list(c) for c in sub["populations"]],
    })
    latex.save_table(tab, f"tbl-app-{t}", f"{TITLES[t]}.  Generated from \\texttt{{config/events.csv}}.",
                     column_format=(r">{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{1.6cm}"
                                    r">{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr\textwidth-6.2cm-4\tabcolsep\relax}"),
                     long=True)
```
