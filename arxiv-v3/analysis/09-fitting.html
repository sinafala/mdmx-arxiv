12.1 What this chapter does

The previous chapters build a model that generates a schedule: a regime \(k\), a life expectancy \(e_0^*\) (chapter 07) and, optionally, a disruption of profile \(\delta_h\) and intensity \(\lambda\) (chapter 08):

\[ \mathbf y = \mathbf z_k(e_0^*) + \lambda\,\boldsymbol\delta_h + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \lambda \ge 0 . \]

Paper §7 runs the model backwards: given one schedule and nothing else — no country, no year — which regime and \(e_0^*\) describe it, and does it carry a disruption? The chapter fits every observed schedule this way, with a generator and profiles built without the schedule’s own population family, and measures how often the fitter is right.

12.2 Step 1 — the fit

For a schedule \(\mathbf y\), regime \(k\) and hypothesis \(h\) (none, or one of the profiles), the fitter minimises the residual sum of squares over the cells it uses,

\[ \mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}(e_0) = \sum_{\text{cells}} \bigl(y - z_k(e_0) - \hat\lambda\,\delta_h\bigr)^2, \qquad \hat\lambda = \max\!\Bigl(0, \frac{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top(\mathbf y - \mathbf z_k)}{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top\boldsymbol\delta_h}\Bigr), \]

over a grid of e₀ 0.1 years apart (each regime’s schedules on the grid are computed once), refined between grid points by a parabola (mdmx.fitting.fit_many). Each fit is turned into a Laplace approximation of its log evidence with a half-normal prior on \(\lambda\) (mdmx.fitting.laplace_log_bf):

\[ \log \mathrm{BF}_{k,h} = \tfrac p2 \log\frac{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,0}}{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}} + \log\frac{2}{\sigma_h\sqrt{2\pi}} - \frac{\hat\lambda^2}{2\sigma_h^2} + \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \tfrac12 \log I_h, \qquad I_h = \frac{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top\boldsymbol\delta_h}{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}/p}, \]

where \(p\) is the number of cells used, \(\sigma_h\) is the prior scale of hypothesis \(h\) (the root mean square of the positive intensities, measured as in chapter 08, of its training country-years), \(\mathrm{RSS}_{k,0}\) is the null model’s own minimum over e₀, and \(\log 2\) is subtracted when \(\hat\lambda = 0\) (the maximum on the boundary). The second term, the prior’s normalising constant, makes a wider prior cost evidence. The best pair over all regimes and hypotheses is the fit; the score of a schedule is the log Bayes factor of its best disrupted fit against its best undisrupted one. Floored cells are always left out; when the exposures are known (as here) the schedule is fitted again without the cells whose deaths expected under its first undisrupted fit are fewer than 5. This is chapter 08’s rule, with its threshold. Only the baseline differs: chapter 08 interpolates the population’s neighbouring years, whereas a single schedule handed to the fitter has none, so its own first fit without disruption serves. The rule never reads a cell’s deaths, although that baseline depends on the schedule as a whole.

The likelihood treats the 220 cells as independent, which they are not; the score is therefore not a calibrated probability. That is why the decision threshold is set on normal schedules (Step 4) rather than at log BF = 0.

12.3 Step 2 — how far a disruption can be told from a change of e₀

A disruption raises mortality at some ages, and so does a lower e₀. The more a profile resembles the direction in which a regime’s schedule moves as e₀ falls — the trajectory tangent \(\mathbf z'_k\), a central difference in the level \(\xi\) — the harder it is to tell the two apart from one schedule (the article’s §7.2). The table gives, over every regime and e₀ in its support, the cosine \(\cos\vartheta\) of the angle between each profile and the tangent, the share of the profile orthogonal to it, \(\lvert\sin\vartheta\rvert\), and the variance inflation \(1/\sin^2\vartheta\).

12.4 Step 3 — every schedule, fitted out of sample

Whole population families are dealt into 10 folds. For each fold the generator (chapter 07’s chosen deviation, mdmx.fitting.fold_model) is estimated from the other folds’ observed country-years, the profiles and prior scales (fold_profiles) from their exceptional ones, and then every schedule of the fold is fitted (mdmx.fitting.evaluate_folds): 4,056 observed normal schedules and 540 exceptional ones, each under both hypothesis sets — one profile per type, or one per historical event with at least 10 training country-years plus the type profiles (always allowed, since the fitter does not know the event). An event whose training country-years are on average a deficit rather than an excess is not a hypothesis. Each schedule is fitted twice (fit_two_pass): without its exposures (only floored cells left out) and with them (cells with few expected deaths left out too); both procedures are calibrated, so that the app can use either.

Three things are not held out, and are stated here: the sex and age bases of the decomposition (chapter 04) and chapter 06’s regime labels come from all data, and the label “detectable” (Step 4) uses chapter 08’s intensities, whose profiles include the country-year itself. The label is never used to fit anything — it only says which disruptions count as visible.

12.5 Step 4 — the decision, and which hypotheses to test

A schedule is called disrupted when its score exceeds a threshold. For the schedules of each fold, the threshold is the score exceeded by only 5 % of the other folds’ normal schedules (mdmx.fitting.cross_fitted_thresholds). A disruption counts as detectable when chapter 08 measured its intensity above its type’s null band (169 of the 534 exceptional country-years of the three types with a profile; the 6 country-years of type “other” are fitted but not scored). The hypothesis set with the higher detection of detectable disruptions at this budget is used — the rule fixed before the run; a tie goes to the type profiles.

def evaluate(set_name, budget, passes="two"):
    """Metrics and flags of one hypothesis set and procedure at one false-positive budget."""
    f = fits[(fits["set"] == set_name) & (fits["passes"] == passes)]
    nr = f[f["kind"] == "normal"].sort_values("row").reset_index(drop=True)
    ex = f[f["kind"] == "exceptional"].sort_values("row").reset_index(drop=True)
    m, n_flag, e_flag = ft.operating_point(nr, ex, budget, truth=types, detectable=detectable,
                                           profile_types=PTYPES, labels=labels, regimes=REGIMES,
                                           e0_data=e0_obs, e0_base=e0_base_exc)
    return m, n_flag, e_flag, nr, ex

At a budget of 5 % of normal schedules (actually flagged out of fold: 5.4 %), the fitter with type profiles detects 49 % of the detectable disruptions (83 of 169): 59 % of wars (60 of 101), 25 % of respiratory (15 of 59) and 89 % of enteric pandemics (8 of 9). Of all war and pandemic years — many of which left no measurable mark — 19 % are detected. Of the detected detectable ones, 78 % get the right type.

The two hypothesis sets are nearly tied: type profiles detect 83 detectable disruptions and event profiles 81 (2 apart), and their ROC curves cross near the budget (figure), with AUCs of 0.82 and 0.84. The rule, which looks at detection only, chose type profiles. Where the two differ is the type: with type profiles 4 of the 15 detected respiratory disruptions get the right type, with event profiles 13 of 18 — COVID-19 and 1918 have profiles of their own.

Respiratory pandemics are the weak point. Almost all of their detectable years belong to two events (Spanish influenza 1918–1920 28, COVID-19 2020–2022 28), of which the fitter detects 11 of 28, 4 of 28: the type profile averages the young-adult excess of 1918 and the old-age excess of COVID-19 (chapter 08), so neither fits it well. Over the whole range of thresholds the trade-off is smooth (figures): from 30 % detected at 1 % false positives to 56 % at 10 %. Of the 217 normal schedules flagged, 55 % are called enteric — the profile least distinguishable from a change of e₀ (Step 2). The threshold itself is a log Bayes factor of about 49, not 0: the likelihood treats the cells of a schedule as independent and the generator does not fit normal schedules perfectly, so the raw score overstates the evidence.

The normal schedules that set the threshold are the observed ones, whose small populations chapter d5 pooled across neighbouring years at the ages where deaths are rare (639 country-years); raw single-year schedules — exceptional years, or a user’s schedule — are noisier. Pooled normal schedules are flagged at 2.5 %, the others at 5.9 %; the false-positive rate of raw schedules from small populations may be higher than the budget. Without exposures (floored cells left out, no expected-deaths rule) and with its own threshold, the fitter flags 5.2 % of normal schedules and detects 49 % of detectable disruptions.

The fit also recovers the schedule’s level and, less reliably, its regime: for normal schedules the fitted e₀* is 1.05 years from the schedule’s own e₀ on average, and the regime is chapter 06’s for 66 % of them. The regimes are a typology of a continuum (chapter 06), and chapter 06 assigns them by the level-free features of a year’s core while the fitter compares whole schedules, so the two need not agree on schedules near the boundaries. For exceptional years, the fitted baseline e₀* is 1.73 years from chapter 08’s baseline on average, against 2.10 years for a fit without disruption: allowing a disruption brings the estimate of the counterfactual e₀ closer.

## What this chapter does

The previous chapters build a model that *generates* a schedule: a regime
$k$, a life expectancy $e_0^*$ (chapter 07) and, optionally, a disruption
of profile $\delta_h$ and intensity $\lambda$ (chapter 08):

$$
\mathbf y = \mathbf z_k(e_0^*) + \lambda\,\boldsymbol\delta_h + \boldsymbol\varepsilon,
\qquad \lambda \ge 0 .
$$

Paper §7 runs the model backwards: given one schedule and nothing else —
no country, no year — which regime and $e_0^*$ describe it, and does it
carry a disruption?  The chapter fits every observed schedule this way,
with a generator and profiles built without the schedule's own population
family, and measures how often the fitter is right.

## Step 1 — the fit

For a schedule $\mathbf y$, regime $k$ and hypothesis $h$ (none, or one of
the profiles), the fitter minimises the residual sum of squares over the
cells it uses,

$$
\mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}(e_0) = \sum_{\text{cells}} \bigl(y - z_k(e_0) - \hat\lambda\,\delta_h\bigr)^2,
\qquad \hat\lambda = \max\!\Bigl(0, \frac{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top(\mathbf y - \mathbf z_k)}{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top\boldsymbol\delta_h}\Bigr),
$$

over a grid of e₀ `{python} f"{PF['grid_step']:g}"` years apart (each
regime's schedules on the grid are computed once), refined between grid
points by a parabola (`mdmx.fitting.fit_many`).  Each fit is turned into a
Laplace approximation of its log evidence with a half-normal prior on
$\lambda$ (`mdmx.fitting.laplace_log_bf`):

$$
\log \mathrm{BF}_{k,h} = \tfrac p2 \log\frac{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,0}}{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}}
+ \log\frac{2}{\sigma_h\sqrt{2\pi}} - \frac{\hat\lambda^2}{2\sigma_h^2}
+ \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \tfrac12 \log I_h,
\qquad I_h = \frac{\boldsymbol\delta_h^\top\boldsymbol\delta_h}{\mathrm{RSS}_{k,h}/p},
$$

where $p$ is the number of cells used, $\sigma_h$ is the prior scale of
hypothesis $h$ (the root mean square of the positive intensities, measured
as in chapter 08, of its training country-years), $\mathrm{RSS}_{k,0}$ is
the null model's own minimum over e₀, and $\log 2$ is subtracted when
$\hat\lambda = 0$ (the maximum on the boundary).  The second term, the
prior's normalising constant, makes a wider prior cost evidence.  The best
pair over all regimes and hypotheses is the fit; the **score** of a
schedule is the log Bayes factor of its best disrupted fit against its
best undisrupted one.  Floored cells are always
left out; when the exposures are known (as here) the schedule is fitted
again without the cells whose deaths expected under its first undisrupted
fit are fewer than `{python} f"{PF['min_expected_deaths']:g}"`.  This is
chapter 08's rule, with its threshold.  Only the baseline differs: chapter
08 interpolates the population's neighbouring years, whereas a single
schedule handed to the fitter has none, so its own first fit without
disruption serves.  The rule never reads a cell's deaths, although that
baseline depends on the schedule as a whole.

The likelihood treats the `{python} f"{S * A}"` cells as independent,
which they are not; the score is therefore not a calibrated probability.
That is why the decision threshold is set on normal schedules (Step 4)
rather than at log BF = 0.

## Step 2 — how far a disruption can be told from a change of e₀

A disruption raises mortality at some ages, and so does a lower e₀.  The
more a profile resembles the direction in which a regime's schedule moves
as e₀ falls — the *trajectory tangent* $\mathbf z'_k$, a central difference in the level $\xi$
— the harder it is to tell the two apart from one schedule (the article's §7.2).
The table gives, over every regime and e₀ in its support, the cosine $\cos\vartheta$ of the angle
between each profile and the tangent, the share of the profile orthogonal
to it, $\lvert\sin\vartheta\rvert$, and the variance inflation $1/\sin^2\vartheta$.

## Step 3 — every schedule, fitted out of sample

Whole population families are dealt into `{python} f"{PF['n_folds']}"`
folds.  For each fold the generator (chapter 07's chosen deviation,
`mdmx.fitting.fold_model`) is estimated from the other folds' observed
country-years, the profiles and prior scales (`fold_profiles`) from their

(`mdmx.fitting.evaluate_folds`): `{python} f"{len(Y_obs):,}"` observed

under both hypothesis sets — one profile per type, or one per historical
event with at least `{python} f"{PROFILES['min_event_n']}"` training
country-years plus the type profiles (always allowed, since the fitter
does not know the event).  An event whose training country-years are on
average a deficit rather than an excess is not a hypothesis.  Each
schedule is fitted twice (`fit_two_pass`): without its exposures (only
floored cells left out) and with them (cells with few expected deaths
left out too); both procedures are calibrated, so that the app can use
either.

bases of the decomposition (chapter 04) and chapter 06's regime labels
come from all data, and the label "detectable" (Step 4) uses chapter 08's
intensities, whose profiles include the country-year itself.  The label
is never used to fit anything — it only says which disruptions count as
visible.

## Step 4 — the decision, and which hypotheses to test

A schedule is called **disrupted** when its score exceeds a threshold.  For
the schedules of each fold, the threshold is the score exceeded by only
`{python} f"{100 * PF['fp_budget']:g}"` % of the *other* folds' normal
schedules (`mdmx.fitting.cross_fitted_thresholds`).  A disruption counts as
**detectable** when chapter 08 measured its intensity above its type's
null band (`{python} num("fitNDetectable")` of the
`{python} num("fitNScored")` exceptional country-years of the three types
with a profile; the `{python} f"{int(np.isin(types, other_types).sum())}"`
country-years of type "other" are fitted but not scored).  The hypothesis
set with the higher detection of detectable disruptions at this budget is
used — the rule fixed before the run; a tie goes to the type profiles.

At a budget of `{python} num("fitBudget")` % of normal schedules (actually
flagged out of fold: `{python} num("fitFp")` %), the fitter with
**`{python} CHOSEN` profiles** detects `{python} num("fitDetect")` % of the
detectable disruptions (`{python} num("fitNDetect")` of
`{python} num("fitNDetectable")`): `{python} num("fitDetectWar")` % of
wars (`{python} num("fitNDetectWar")` of `{python} num("fitNDetectableWar")`),
`{python} num("fitDetectResp")` % of respiratory
(`{python} num("fitNDetectResp")` of `{python} num("fitNDetectableResp")`)
and `{python} num("fitDetectEnteric")` % of enteric pandemics
(`{python} num("fitNDetectEnteric")` of `{python} num("fitNDetectableEnteric")`).
Of all war and pandemic years — many of which left no measurable mark —
`{python} num("fitDetectAll")` % are detected.  Of the detected detectable
ones, `{python} num("fitTypeAcc")` % get the right type.

The two hypothesis sets are nearly tied: `{python} CHOSEN` profiles detect
`{python} f"{r5['n_detect']}"` detectable disruptions and
`{python} OTHER` profiles `{python} f"{ro['n_detect']}"`
(`{python} f"{_tied}"` apart), and their ROC curves cross near the budget
(figure), with AUCs of `{python} f"{auc[CHOSEN][2]:.2f}"` and
`{python} f"{auc[OTHER][2]:.2f}"`.  The rule, which looks at detection only,
chose `{python} CHOSEN` profiles.  Where the two differ is the *type*:
with type profiles `{python} num(f"fitNTypeRight{WORD[RESP]}Type")` of the
`{python} num(f"fitNTypeDen{WORD[RESP]}Type")` detected respiratory
disruptions get the right type, with event profiles
`{python} num(f"fitNTypeRight{WORD[RESP]}Event")` of
`{python} num(f"fitNTypeDen{WORD[RESP]}Event")` — COVID-19 and 1918 have
profiles of their own.

Respiratory pandemics are the weak point.  Almost all of their detectable
years belong to two events (`{python} _resp_top`), of which the fitter
detects `{python} _resp_det`: the type profile averages the young-adult
excess of 1918 and the old-age excess of COVID-19 (chapter 08), so neither
fits it well.  Over the whole range of thresholds the trade-off is smooth
(figures): from `{python} num("fitDetectLow")` % detected at
`{python} f"{100 * min(OPS):g}"` % false positives to
`{python} num("fitDetectHigh")` % at `{python} f"{100 * max(OPS):g}"` %.
Of the `{python} num("fitNFlaggedNormal")` normal schedules flagged,
`{python} num("fitFlaggedAsTop")` % are called
`{python} _flag_text`.  The threshold itself is a log Bayes factor of about
`{python} num("fitThreshold")`, not 0: the likelihood treats the cells of a
schedule as independent and the generator does not fit normal schedules
perfectly, so the raw score overstates the evidence.

The normal schedules that set the threshold are the observed ones, whose
small populations chapter d5 pooled across neighbouring years at the ages
where deaths are rare (`{python} num("fitNPooled")` country-years); raw
single-year schedules — exceptional years, or a user's schedule — are
noisier.  Pooled normal schedules are flagged at
`{python} num("fitFpPooled")` %, the others at `{python} num("fitFpUnpooled")` %;
the false-positive rate of raw schedules from small populations may be
higher than the budget.  Without exposures (floored cells left out, no
expected-deaths rule) and with its own threshold, the fitter flags
`{python} num("fitFpNoExposure")` % of normal schedules and detects
`{python} num("fitDetectNoExposure")` % of detectable disruptions.

The fit also recovers the schedule's level and, less reliably, its
regime: for normal schedules the fitted e₀* is
`{python} num("fitEzeroMaeNormal")` years from the schedule's own e₀ on
average, and the regime is chapter 06's for `{python} num("fitRegimeAgree")` %
of them.  The regimes are a typology of a continuum (chapter 06), and
chapter 06 assigns them by the level-free features of a year's core
while the fitter compares whole schedules, so the two need not agree on
schedules near the boundaries.  For exceptional years, the fitted
baseline e₀* is `{python} num("fitEzeroMaeExc")` years from chapter 08's
baseline on average, against `{python} num("fitEzeroMaeExcNull")` years
for a fit without disruption: allowing a disruption brings the estimate of
the counterfactual e₀ closer.
