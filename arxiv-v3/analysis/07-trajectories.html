10.1 What this chapter does

A model life table system must produce a complete schedule of mortality at any level, including levels not observed in any country-year. Paper §5 does this for a regime \(k\) and a target life expectancy \(x\). Life expectancy alone does not determine a schedule — infinitely many schedules share an e₀ (paper §5.1) — so the regime supplies the shape.

Chapter 06 defines a regime as a deviation pattern from the typical schedule at the same e₀. The natural generator is therefore

\[ \mathcal{G}_k(x) \;=\; \bar{\mathcal{G}}(x) + \mathbf{D}_k(x), \qquad \mathbf{z}_k(x) \;=\; \mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{G}_k(x)\,\mathbf{A}^\top , \]

the typical effective core at \(x\) — the e₀-conditional mean of chapter 06, whose schedule is the paper’s typical schedule \(\bar{\mathcal{M}}(x)\) (Eq. 10) — plus the regime’s deviation, mapped to logit(\(q_x\)) for both sexes (the paper’s trajectory of a regime, Eq. 13 in §5.2). The chapter asks how the deviation should depend on \(x\), answers by a hold-out over populations, and then examines what the resulting trajectories show: the sex differential, the change in the age pattern as e₀ rises, and how well a schedule’s regime and e₀ can be recovered from the schedule.

10.2 Step 1 — the typical schedule and the level calibration

The typical effective core \(\bar{\mathcal{G}}(x)\) is chapter 06’s e₀-conditional mean: a LOWESS of every core entry against the e₀ of the observed schedules (span 0.1), stored on a grid of 662 e₀ values from 19.9 to 86.0. Its schedule’s e₀ is close to \(x\) but not equal to it: the mean of the cores at a given e₀ is not a core with exactly that e₀, because the life table is not linear in logit(\(q_x\)). mdmx.trajectories.calibrate_level therefore shifts the level coordinate \(\mathcal{G}[1,1]\), which moves logit(\(q_x\)) up or down at almost every age at once, until the schedule’s sex-average e₀ equals \(x\); the coordinates that carry the shape are left alone. The shift is found by bisection, and the step stops with an error rather than returning an uncalibrated schedule if the target cannot be bracketed.

10.3 Step 2 — the deviation, chosen by a hold-out over populations

How should a regime’s deviation depend on the level of mortality? The candidates:

  • typical — no deviation: every regime gets the typical schedule (the baseline: the typical schedule of all populations, paper Eq. 10);
  • constant — the regime’s mean deviation at every e₀ (the definition of a regime in chapter 06);
  • LOWESS — a LOWESS of the regime members’ features against e₀, so that the deviation changes with the level of mortality (a per-regime trajectory in level-free form), with span 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1 of the regime’s members;
  • neural — one small network for all regimes from the regime and e₀ to the features (mdmx.nn; settings in the parameter file), trained with early stopping on whole held-out families.

Each of the last three is tried on chapter 06’s feature positions alone and with the sex contrast of the level column added (mdmx.trajectories.deviation_positions). Chapter 06 builds the regimes without the level column, so on its features alone a regime keeps its shape but takes the typical overall sex differential; the sex contrast of the level column — how far apart male and female mortality sit overall — lets it keep its own: without it, the female advantage of the Central-Eastern European regime comes out too small (Step 3 measures by how much). Adding the sex contrast to chapter 06’s clustering features instead turned the regimes into eras, so it enters through the deviation. Outside a regime’s central e₀ range (its support: the 5–95 % range of its members) a deviation is held at its value at the boundary.

The candidates are compared on schedules the fits have not seen. Whole population families — a population with its HMD parts, because the years of one population are not independent — are dealt into 10 folds (svdcomp.assign_folds). For each fold, the typical schedule and every candidate are estimated from the other folds; each held-out country-year’s schedule is then generated from its regime at its observed e₀, calibrated to that e₀, and compared with the observed schedule: the RMSE of logit(\(q_x\)) over both sexes and ages 0–109 (mdmx.trajectories.holdout_scores). The rule, fixed before the run: the candidate with the lowest pooled held-out RMSE over the non-noise regimes is used. The deviations are estimated from the non-noise regimes only, as in the model the chapter keeps; the noise cluster is scored in a separate run (without the neural candidate) for information. The regime labels come from chapter 06’s clustering of all data, so the hold-out asks how well a schedule is rebuilt given its regime — as in an application where the user chooses the regime. The LOWESS span is the best of its grid on the same folds, which favours the LOWESS slightly.

Each held-out country-year above is rebuilt with the regime the clustering gave it, and the clustering saw that year’s own schedule: the gain over the typical schedule is how much the regimes describe, given the regime. When the regime is not known it must be guessed without the schedule. Here each held-out country-year is given the regime of the nearest country-year of the other folds in e₀ and calendar year (each standardised by its standard deviation; mdmx.trajectories.nearest_regimes), and the chosen deviation is scored again on the same rows.

10.7 Step 6 — does the age pattern of decline rotate?

Paper §5.2 motivates the trajectories by the rotation of mortality decline — as e₀ rises, gains shift from young to old ages — and §5.4 measures it. We examine it along the typical schedule in two ways (mdmx.trajectories.age_contributions): the slope of logit(\(q_x\)) per year of e₀ at three levels — the paper’s measure — and the share of a gain in e₀ that comes from each age group, from the exact derivative of e₀ with respect to logit(\(q_x\)) times that slope. Both use central differences over ±2.5 years of e₀, wide enough not to follow the small irregularities of the typical schedule (chapter 06’s short smoothing span follows the mix of populations observed at each level); over such a step the shares are rescaled to add up to one.

## What this chapter does

A model life table system must produce a complete schedule of mortality
at *any* level, including levels not observed in any country-year.  Paper
§5 does this for a regime $k$ and a target life expectancy $x$.  Life
expectancy alone does not determine a schedule — infinitely many
schedules share an e₀ (paper §5.1) — so the regime supplies the shape.

Chapter 06 defines a regime as a **deviation pattern from the typical
schedule at the same e₀**.  The natural generator is therefore

$$
\mathcal{G}_k(x) \;=\; \bar{\mathcal{G}}(x) + \mathbf{D}_k(x),
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_k(x) \;=\; \mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{G}_k(x)\,\mathbf{A}^\top ,
$$

the typical effective core at $x$ — the e₀-conditional mean of chapter 06,
whose schedule is the paper's typical schedule $\bar{\mathcal{M}}(x)$ (Eq. 10) — plus the
regime's deviation, mapped to logit($q_x$) for both sexes (the paper's trajectory of a
regime, Eq. 13 in §5.2).  The chapter
asks how the deviation should depend on $x$, answers by a hold-out over
populations, and then examines what the resulting trajectories show:
the sex differential, the change in the age pattern as e₀ rises, and how
well a schedule's regime and e₀ can be recovered from the schedule.

## Step 1 — the typical schedule and the level calibration

The typical effective core $\bar{\mathcal{G}}(x)$ is chapter 06's
e₀-conditional mean: a LOWESS of every core entry against the e₀ of the
observed schedules (span `{python} f"{PF['conditional_frac']}"`), stored on
a grid of `{python} f"{len(grid)}"` e₀ values from
`{python} f"{grid[0]:.1f}"` to `{python} f"{grid[-1]:.1f}"`.  Its schedule's
e₀ is close to $x$ but not equal to it: the mean of the cores at a given
e₀ is not a core with exactly that e₀, because the life table is not
linear in logit($q_x$).  `mdmx.trajectories.calibrate_level` therefore
shifts the **level coordinate** $\mathcal{G}[1,1]$, which moves logit($q_x$)
up or down at almost every age at once, until the schedule's sex-average
e₀ equals $x$; the coordinates that carry the shape are left alone.  The
shift is found by bisection, and the step stops with an error rather than
returning an uncalibrated schedule if the target cannot be bracketed.

```{python}
#| label: c07-typical
```

Before calibration the typical schedule's e₀ is between
`{python} f"{typ_gap.min():+.2f}"` and `{python} f"{typ_gap.max():+.2f}"`
years from the target, `{python} "above" if typ_gap.mean() > 0 else "below"`
it on average (`{python} f"{typ_gap.mean():+.2f}"`): a schedule averaged in
logit($q_x$) does not have the average e₀ of the schedules it averages.
After calibration the e₀ is exact.

## Step 2 — the deviation, chosen by a hold-out over populations

How should a regime's deviation depend on the level of mortality?  The
candidates:

* **typical** — no deviation: every regime gets the typical schedule (the
  baseline: the typical schedule of all populations, paper Eq. 10);
* **constant** — the regime's mean deviation at every e₀ (the definition of
  a regime in chapter 06);
* **LOWESS** — a LOWESS of the regime members' features against e₀, so that
  the deviation changes with the level of mortality (a per-regime
  trajectory in level-free form), with span
  `{python} ", ".join(f"{f:g}" for f in PT["lowess_fracs"])` of the
  regime's members;
* **neural** — one small network for all regimes from the regime and e₀ to
  the features (`mdmx.nn`; settings in the parameter file), trained with
  early stopping on whole held-out families.

Each of the last three is tried on chapter 06's feature positions alone
and **with the sex contrast of the level column** added
(`mdmx.trajectories.deviation_positions`).  Chapter 06 builds the regimes
without the level column, so on its features alone a regime keeps its
shape but takes the typical overall sex differential; the sex contrast of
the level column — how far apart male and female mortality sit overall —
lets it keep its own: without it, the female advantage of the Central-Eastern European regime
comes out too small (Step 3 measures by how much).  Adding the sex contrast to chapter 06's
clustering features instead turned the regimes into eras, so it enters
through the deviation.  Outside a regime's central e₀ range (its *support*: the
`{python} f"{100 * PT['support_quantiles'][0]:.0f}"`–`{python} f"{100 * PT['support_quantiles'][1]:.0f}"`
% range of its members) a deviation is held at its value at the boundary.

The candidates are compared on schedules the fits have not seen.  Whole
population families — a population with its HMD parts, because the years
of one population are not independent — are dealt into
`{python} f"{PT['n_folds']}"` folds (`svdcomp.assign_folds`).  For each fold,
the typical schedule and every candidate are estimated from the other
folds; each held-out country-year's schedule is then generated from its
regime at its observed e₀, calibrated to that e₀, and compared with the
observed schedule: the **RMSE of logit($q_x$)** over both sexes and ages
0–109 (`mdmx.trajectories.holdout_scores`).  The rule, fixed before the
run: **the candidate with the lowest pooled held-out RMSE over the
non-noise regimes is used**.  The deviations are estimated from the
non-noise regimes only, as in the model the chapter keeps; the noise
cluster is scored in a separate run (without the neural candidate) for
information.  The regime labels come from chapter 06's clustering of all
data, so the hold-out asks how well a schedule is rebuilt *given* its
regime — as in an application where the user chooses the regime.  The
LOWESS span is the best of its grid on the same folds, which favours the
LOWESS slightly.

```{python}
#| label: c07-holdout
#| code-summary: "The hold-out over population families (cached; a few minutes)"
def cand_name(kind, frac=None, sex=False):
    """Hold-out name of a candidate, e.g. 'lowess 0.8 + sex level'."""
    base = f"lowess {frac:g}" if kind == "lowess" else kind
    return base + (" + sex level" if sex else "")


if sorted(PT["sex_level_options"]) != [False, True]:
    raise ValueError("sex_level_options must be [false, true]: the chapter compares each deviation with and "
                     "without the sex level.")
CANDIDATES = {"typical": {"kind": "typical"}}
for sex in PT["sex_level_options"]:
    CANDIDATES[cand_name("constant", sex=sex)] = {"kind": "constant", "sex_level": sex}
    for f in PT["lowess_fracs"]:
        CANDIDATES[cand_name("lowess", f, sex)] = {"kind": "lowess", "frac": f, "sex_level": sex}
    CANDIDATES[cand_name("neural", sex=sex)] = {"kind": "neural", "sex_level": sex, **PT["neural"]}
folds = sc.assign_folds(family, PT["n_folds"], PT["fold_seed"])
HOLDOUT = {"feature_index": fidx, "grid": grid, "typical": TYPICAL, "U_sex": U_sex, "U_age": U_age,
           "bracket": BRACKET, "support_quantiles": PT["support_quantiles"]}


def _holdout():
    main_runs = tr.holdout_scores(G_obs, z_obs, e0, labels, family, folds, REGIMES, CANDIDATES, **HOLDOUT)
    noise_cands = {n_: s_ for n_, s_ in CANDIDATES.items() if s_["kind"] != "neural"}
    noise_runs = (tr.holdout_scores(G_obs, z_obs, e0, labels, family, folds, NOISE, noise_cands, **HOLDOUT)
                  if NOISE else pd.DataFrame(columns=main_runs.columns))
    return {"main": main_runs, "noise": noise_runs}


holdout = artifacts.stage(
    "trajectories_holdout", _holdout, inputs=["clustering", "tucker", "tensor", "populations"],
    params={"candidates": CANDIDATES, "folds": {"n": PT["n_folds"], "seed": PT["fold_seed"]},
            "typical": TYPICAL, "bracket": BRACKET, "support": PT["support_quantiles"]},
    code=[_holdout, cand_name, tr, clus, sc.assign_folds, sc.population_families, mnn, lifetable],
    desc="Schedules of held-out population families rebuilt from regime and e0, for each candidate deviation")
main = holdout["main"]
pooled = main.groupby("candidate", sort=False)["mse"].mean().pow(0.5)
by_regime = main.pivot_table(index="candidate", columns="regime", values="mse", aggfunc="mean").pow(0.5)
noise_rmse = holdout["noise"].groupby("candidate")["mse"].mean().pow(0.5)
gap_mae = main.groupby("candidate")["e0_gap"].apply(lambda g: g.abs().mean())
BEST = str(pooled.idxmin())
CHOSEN = CANDIDATES[BEST]
SEX = bool(CHOSEN.get("sex_level", False))


def best_of(kind, sex):
    """The best-scoring candidate of a kind (over spans for the LOWESS), with or without the sex level."""
    names_ = [n_ for n_, s_ in CANDIDATES.items() if s_["kind"] == kind and s_.get("sex_level", False) == sex]
    return str(pooled[names_].idxmin())


print(pd.DataFrame({"pooled RMSE": pooled, "|e0 gap| before calibration": gap_mae}).round(4).to_string())
print(f"\nChosen: {BEST}")
```

clustering saw that year's own schedule: the gain over the typical schedule is how much the
regimes describe, given the regime.  When the regime is not known it must be guessed without the
schedule.  Here each held-out country-year is given the regime of the nearest country-year of the
other folds in e₀ and calendar year (each standardised by its standard deviation;
`mdmx.trajectories.nearest_regimes`), and the chosen deviation is scored again on the same rows.

```{python}
def _model():
    positions = tr.deviation_positions(fidx, U_sex.shape[1], sex_level=SEX)
    typical_core = clusters_art["conditional_mean"]["core"]
    # Residuals from the typical schedule's LOWESS values at the data, as in the hold-out (chapter 06's
```

## Step 6 — does the age pattern of decline rotate?

Paper §5.2 motivates the trajectories by the **rotation** of mortality
decline — as e₀ rises, gains shift from young to old ages — and §5.4 measures it.
We examine it
along the typical schedule in two ways
(`mdmx.trajectories.age_contributions`): the slope of logit($q_x$)
per year of e₀ at three levels — the paper's measure — and the **share of a
gain in e₀ that comes from each age group**, from the exact derivative of
e₀ with respect to logit($q_x$) times that slope.  Both use central
differences over ±`{python} f"{PT['rotation_step']:g}"` years of e₀, wide
enough not to follow the small irregularities of the typical schedule
(chapter 06's short smoothing span follows the mix of populations observed
at each level); over such a step the shares are rescaled to add up to one.

## Step 7 — from a schedule back to its regime and e₀

Chapter 09 fits model schedules to data, which needs the inverse of the
generator (paper §5.1; the results below are in the companion only): the regime and e₀
whose generated schedule is closest to a
given schedule (`mdmx.trajectories.invert`: for each regime, a search over
e₀ for the smallest RMSE of logit($q_x$), then the best regime).  A random
sample of `{python} f"{PT['invert_sample']}"` observed country-years of the
non-noise regimes shows how well this recovers their regime and e₀.  It is
an in-sample check: the model and the regime labels were estimated from
these same schedules.

```{python}
#| label: c07-invert
#| code-summary: "Invert a sample of observed schedules (cached)"
def _invert():
    pool = np.flatnonzero(np.isin(labels, REGIMES))
    rng = np.random.default_rng(PT["invert_seed"])
    sample = np.sort(rng.choice(pool, size=min(PT["invert_sample"], pool.size), replace=False))
    rows = []
    for i in sample:
        found = tr.invert(z_obs[i], model, bracket=BRACKET, step=PT["invert_step"])
        rows.append({"row": int(i), "regime": int(labels[i]), "found": found["regime"],
                     "e0": float(e0[i]), "e0_found": found["e0"], "rmse": found["rmse"]})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


inv = artifacts.stage(
    "trajectories_inversion", _invert, inputs=["clustering", "tucker", "tensor", "trajectories"],
    params={"sample": PT["invert_sample"], "seed": PT["invert_seed"], "step": PT["invert_step"],
            "bracket": BRACKET, "regimes": REGIMES},
    code=[_invert, tr, clus, lifetable],
    desc="Regime and e0 recovered from a sample of observed schedules")
pool_all = np.isin(labels, REGIMES)
agree = float(np.mean(inv["found"] == inv["regime"]))
chance = float(np.max(np.bincount(labels[pool_all]) / pool_all.sum()))
e0_err = inv["e0_found"] - inv["e0"]
print(pd.crosstab(inv["regime"] + 1, inv["found"] + 1, rownames=["labelled"], colnames=["recovered"]))
print(f"\nRegime recovered for {100 * agree:.0f}% (largest regime's share: {100 * chance:.0f}%); "
      f"e0 error mean {e0_err.mean():+.2f}, mean absolute {e0_err.abs().mean():.2f} years")
latex.report("trajInvertAgree", 100 * agree, digits=0, desc="Percent of sampled schedules whose regime is recovered by inversion")
latex.report("trajInvertChance", 100 * chance, digits=0, desc="Share of the largest regime among non-noise country-years (percent)")
latex.report("trajInvertEzeroMae", float(e0_err.abs().mean()), digits=2,
             desc="Mean absolute difference between the e0 of the best-matching generated schedule and the schedule's own e0 (years)")
```

The regime is recovered for `{python} f"{100 * agree:.0f}"`% of the sampled
schedules, against `{python} f"{100 * chance:.0f}"`% for always guessing the
largest regime.  The regimes are summaries of a continuum of deviation
patterns (chapter 06), so a single schedule's regime can be ambiguous.
The e₀ of the best-matching generated schedule differs from the schedule's
own e₀ by `{python} f"{e0_err.abs().mean():.2f}"` years on average: the
closest schedule in logit($q_x$) is not always the one with the same life
expectancy.  Whether a fit should match the schedule or fix e₀ to the
data's is a question for chapter 09.

# The `trajectories` artefact

| Key | Shape / type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| `kind`, `candidate` | str | the chosen deviation (`lowess`, …) and its hold-out name |
| `e0_grid` | (g,) | e₀ grid (chapter 06) |
| `typical` | (g, r₁, r₂) | typical effective core on the grid |
| `deviation` | (K′, g, p) | each regime's deviation at its positions, on the grid (constant outside the support) |
| `support` | (K′, 2) | each regime's e₀ support |
| `clusters` | (K′,) int | the regimes served (chapter 06 labels; the noise cluster is not among them) |
| `feature_index` | (p, 2) | (sex component, age column) of each deviation entry: chapter 06's features plus the sex contrast of the level column if the chosen deviation uses it — *not* always `clustering["features"]["index"]` |
| `U_sex`, `U_age` | (S, r₁), (A, r₂) | the bases |
| `holdout_rmse` | DataFrame | pooled held-out RMSE of every candidate |
