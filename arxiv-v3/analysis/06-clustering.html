9.1 What this chapter does

The decomposition of chapter 04 represents every observed schedule by its effective core \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\), a \(2 \times r_2\) matrix of coordinates in the shared sex and age bases. Paper §4 uses these coordinates to sort country-years into a typology of mortality age patterns; each type is called a regime, here and in the chapters that use it. A regime is a deviation pattern relative to the typical schedule at the same life expectancy: two country-years belong to the same cluster when their schedules depart in the same way from the schedule that is typical for their e₀. Level is removed twice over: the level coordinate (the first age column of \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\)) is dropped, and from every remaining coordinate we subtract its average at the country-year’s e₀.

Part A chooses which part of the effective core carries the shape signal, builds the level-free features, fits Gaussian mixtures for \(K = 2, \ldots, 15\), chooses the largest \(K\) whose partition is reproducible (reporting BIC and how the clusters fare when populations are resampled), cross-checks with Ward’s method, and describes the clusters — their deviation patterns, members, relation to level, time and place, and whether any of them merely collects noisy schedules of small populations. Part B classifies how the level changes within each country (it does not use the clustering features). The artefact saved at the end lets later chapters build a schedule for any cluster at any e₀.

10.2 Step 2 — the smooth, low-order part of the effective core

The schedule implied by an effective core is a sum of terms, one per entry of \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\) (paper §4.1): the first age column multiplies \(\mathbf{a}_1\), which is close to the average logit(\(q_x\)) schedule, and so carries the level; the later columns carry the shape. Not all shape columns are equally useful. The age basis has \(r_2\) = 50 columns because the decomposition must reproduce every observed schedule almost exactly (chapter 04), including the year-to-year jitter of single-year rates in small populations. That jitter lives in the high-order columns. Clustering on them groups country-years by how noisy their data are, not by how their mortality differs.

We therefore keep only the first \(c_{\text{feat}}\) age columns, with \(c_{\text{feat}}\) the smallest number of age components that together capture a share τ = 0.999 of the sum of squares of the observed schedules — the τ rule that chapter 04 uses for \(r_2\), at a less demanding threshold (mdmx.clustering.column_energy_share, choose_n_columns). Two further measures, computed for every column, show whether the rule separates signal from noise:

  • the roughness of the basis function \(\mathbf{a}_j\) — the sum of its squared second differences over ages 2–109 (basis_roughness): smooth age patterns have values near zero;
  • the year-to-year noise ratio of each core entry (year_to_year_noise): the mean squared change between consecutive observed years of a population, divided by twice the entry’s overall variance. An entry that moves smoothly over time scores near 0; one whose values are independent from year to year scores near 1.

The rule gives \(c_{\text{feat}}\) = 6, and the two other measures break at the same place. The basis functions of columns 2–6 are smooth (roughness at most 0.003), while those from column 7 on are at least 0.06 — tens to thousands of times rougher. The sex-average entries of columns 1–6 change smoothly over time (noise ratio at most 0.12), while those of the next five columns average 0.40. The sex-difference entries are noisier throughout — the sex gap of a small population is estimated from few deaths — but are kept, because the sex pattern is part of what distinguishes mortality regimes; Step 8 checks whether this lets noise drive any cluster.

10.3 Step 3 — level-free features

The features are built from the entries of columns 2–6 of \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\), for both sex components: the level column is dropped. Dropping the level column is not enough to make the remaining coordinates independent of level, because the shape of the schedule changes systematically as mortality falls (the “rotation” of the age pattern of decline; paper §4.1). So, for each entry \(m\), we estimate its e₀-conditional mean \(\mu_m(e_0)\) — a LOWESS curve of the entry against e₀ fitted across all observed country-years, with a span of 0.1 of the points and 3 robustifying iterations (mdmx.clustering.conditional_mean) — and subtract it:

\[ f_{c,t,m} \;=\; \mathcal{G}_{c,t}[i_m, j_m] \;-\; \mu_m\!\left(e_{0,c,t}\right), \qquad j_m \in \{2, \ldots, k\},\; i_m \in \{1, 2\}. \]

The feature vector \(\mathbf{f}_{c,t}\) (length \(2(k-1)\) = 10) says how this country-year’s schedule departs from the schedule that is typical at its e₀. The same LOWESS is applied to every entry of the effective core, including the level column and the columns beyond \(c_{\text{feat}}\); this conditional-mean core \(\bar{\mathcal{G}}(e_0)\) is stored on a grid of e₀ values (step 0.1 years) so that later chapters can build the typical schedule \(\mathbf{S}\bar{\mathcal{G}}(e_0)\mathbf{A}^\top\) at any e₀.

10.5 Step 5 — Gaussian mixtures and the choice of K

A Gaussian mixture model (paper §4.2) treats the rows of the reduced feature matrix as draws from \(K\) multivariate normal distributions, each with its own mean vector and its own unrestricted (“full”) covariance matrix. It is fitted by EM for each \(K\) = 2, …, 15, with 20 EM starts from k-means solutions (seed in the parameter file). The usual choice of \(K\) minimises

\[ \mathrm{BIC}(K) = -2\,\ell(\hat\theta_K) + p_K \log n_{\text{obs}}, \qquad p_K = (K-1) + K d + K\,\tfrac{d(d+1)}{2} \]

(mdmx.clustering.bic_sweep checks the formula against scikit-learn). Shared (“tied”) and diagonal covariance matrices are compared in the supplementary section. Clusters are numbered by increasing mean e₀ of their members (order_clusters), which makes the numbering reproducible.

Reproducibility. The likelihood of a mixture has many local maxima, and EM started from other k-means solutions can end at a different partition of almost the same likelihood. For every \(K\) the mixture is refitted from 5 seeds — the chapter’s and those listed under seeds in the parameter file, each with 20 EM starts — and the partitions are compared in pairs with the adjusted Rand index (ARI: 1 = identical, 0 = chance agreement). The chapter uses the largest \(K\) whose refits agree at a mean ARI of at least 0.9, as do the refits of every smaller \(K\) (mdmx.clustering.choose_k_reproducible); its partition is that \(K\)’s fit from the sweep above.

Resampling of populations. Reproducibility is a property of the method on these data. Whether the clusters would also appear in another set of populations is a different question. Following Hennig (2007), we resample whole population families — a population together with its HMD parts, such as the United Kingdom with England & Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland (svdcomp.population_families), because the years of one population are not independent — with replacement, refit the mixture to each of 50 resamples (each with the 20 EM starts of the chapter’s partition, so that the check measures the populations and not the starting values), and record for every cluster its Jaccard similarity with its best match in the refit, on the country-years in the resample (mdmx.clustering.group_bootstrap, cluster_jaccard). When whole population families are resampled, no \(K\) keeps every cluster stable (mean Jaccard ≥ 0.75); the best is \(K\) = 2, whose least stable cluster reaches 0.60. If anything the check is lenient, because the typical schedule (Step 3) and the PCA are fitted to all populations and held fixed; only the mixture is refitted. The clusters are a reproducible description of these 48 populations — a typology in the spirit of the regional model life tables of Coale and Demeny (1966) — not regimes that any set of populations would reproduce.

10.7 Step 7 — what the clusters are

Each cluster is summarised by its mean deviation \(\bar{\mathbf{f}}_k\), the mean of its members’ features (mdmx.clustering.cluster_means). Mapped back to core coordinates (features_to_cores: the feature entries filled in, zeros elsewhere) it is a matrix \(\mathbf{D}_k\), and the cluster’s schedule at any life expectancy \(x\) is

\[ \mathcal{M}_k(x) \;=\; \mathbf{S}\,\bigl[\bar{\mathcal{G}}(x) + \mathbf{D}_k\bigr]\,\mathbf{A}^\top, \]

the typical schedule at \(x\) plus the cluster’s deviation \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{D}_k\mathbf{A}^\top\), which is the same at every \(x\). The members’ own schedules, and their e₀, vary widely within a cluster; what they share is the deviation.

Where the data allow a test, the clusters are not level strata: every 5-year e₀ band with at least 100 country-years contains at least 3 clusters, and the bands between e₀ = 65 and 80 contain 4–5 of the 5. Cluster membership explains \(\eta^2\) = 0.23 of the variance of e₀ and \(\eta^2\) = 0.25 of the variance of calendar year. This is not level leaking back into the features, whose mean does not depend on e₀ (Step 3). What association there is comes from composition — below e₀ ≈ 45 only a few populations with long early series are observed, so the deviation patterns found there are theirs, and populations that enter the HMD late are observed only at high e₀ — and from spread: the residuals are larger at high mortality, and clusters that differ mainly in how widely their members scatter (see Step 7) differ in e₀ and period with it. The clusters are therefore free of level in the sense intended — they are defined by deviations from the typical schedule at the same e₀ — but they are not uncorrelated with level or period.

11 Part B — Level epochs

11.1 The level signal and its direction

The level coordinate \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1]\) removed in Part A is used here to describe how fast mortality is changing (the level epochs, which are in the companion only). Its sign is that of the singular vectors \(\mathbf{s}_1\) and \(\mathbf{a}_1\), which is a convention: in this decomposition \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1]\) falls as e₀ rises, but another linear-algebra library or sign rule could flip it. To make “improvement” mean improvement whatever the convention, we multiply the level coordinate by the sign of its correlation with e₀ (mdmx.clustering.level_orientation):

\[ \ell_{c,t} = \sigma\, \mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1], \qquad \sigma = \operatorname{sign}\operatorname{corr}\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1],\, e_0\bigr), \]

so that \(\ell\) rises when mortality improves whatever the orientation of the bases (with the decomposition chapter’s orientation, \(\sigma = +1\)). The thresholds below are stated for \(\ell\). To express them in years of life expectancy, we regress e₀ on \(\ell\) across all observed country-years: one unit of \(\ell\) corresponds to \(b\) years of e₀.

11.2 The classification

The procedure takes every setting from the parameter file:

  1. Smooth. For each population with at least 15 observed years, smooth \(\ell\) and e₀ over its observed years by LOWESS (mdmx.clustering.lowess_at), with a span of 0.2 of the population’s years but never fewer than 10 years, and 3 robustness iterations.
  2. Slopes. Slide a window of \(W\) = 15 calendar years across the series one year at a time; in each window with at least 8 observed years, fit a least-squares line to the smoothed \(\ell\) and keep its slope (mdmx.clustering.window_slopes).
  3. Classify each window slope \(s\): rapid improvement if \(s > \delta_{\text{rapid}}\), slow improvement if \(\delta < s \le \delta_{\text{rapid}}\), stagnation if \(|s| \le \delta\), slow worsening if \(-\delta_{\text{rapid}} \le s < -\delta\), rapid worsening if \(s < -\delta_{\text{rapid}}\) (classify_slopes).
  4. Assign years. Each calendar year from the population’s first to last observed year takes the most common category among the windows that contain it (yearly_categories, which uses majority_label); ties go to the category listed first above, i.e. towards rapid improvement. Years in the middle of a series are covered by \(W\) windows; years near either end by fewer, so their category rests on less evidence.
  5. Epochs are maximal runs of consecutive calendar years with the same category (runs).

def _variant_features(n_columns, frac, standardise):
    """Level-free features for one variant of the specification."""
    idx_v = clus.feature_index(r1, n_columns)
    raw_v = clus.cores_to_features(G_obs, idx_v)
    cm_v = {**cm, "frac": frac}
    fit_v, _ = clus.conditional_mean(e0, raw_v, **cm_v)
    res_v = raw_v - fit_v
    if standardise:
        scale2, _ = clus.conditional_mean(e0, res_v ** 2, **cm_v)
        res_v = res_v / np.sqrt(np.clip(scale2, 1e-12, None))
    return res_v

## What this chapter does

The decomposition of chapter 04 represents every observed schedule by its
effective core $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}$, a $2 \times r_2$ matrix of coordinates in
the shared sex and age bases.  Paper §4 uses these coordinates to sort
country-years into a **typology of mortality age patterns**; each type is
called a **regime**, here and in the chapters that use it.  A regime is a
**deviation pattern relative to the typical schedule at the same life
expectancy**: two country-years belong to the same cluster when their
schedules depart in the same way from the schedule that is typical for
their e₀.  Level is removed twice over: the level coordinate (the first age
column of $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}$) is dropped, and from every remaining
coordinate we subtract its average at the country-year's e₀.

**Part A** chooses which part of the effective core carries the shape
signal, builds the level-free features, fits Gaussian mixtures for
$K = 2, \ldots, 15$, chooses the largest $K$ whose partition is reproducible
(reporting BIC and how the clusters fare when populations are resampled),
cross-checks with Ward's method, and describes the clusters — their deviation patterns, members, relation to
level, time and place, and whether any of them merely collects noisy
schedules of small populations.  **Part B** classifies how the *level*
changes within each country (it does not use the clustering features).
The artefact saved at the end lets later chapters build a schedule for
any cluster at any e₀.

# Part A — Level-free clusters

## Step 1 — the unit of clustering

The objects clustered are **country-years**: each observed pair $(c, t)$
with $O_{c,t} = 1$ is one data point (paper §4.1).  Country-years without
data, and exceptional years set aside for the disruption model, are not
clustered.  `mdmx.clustering.observed_index` lists them in a fixed order (by
population, then year); every per-observation array in this chapter uses
that order.  Each country-year's life expectancy at birth is computed from
its **observed** schedule with the project's single life-table function and
averaged over the sexes; it is checked against the HMD's published e₀ and
against the e₀ of the schedule implied by the effective core.

## Step 2 — the smooth, low-order part of the effective core

The schedule implied by an effective core is a sum of terms, one per entry
of $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}$ (paper §4.1): the
first age column multiplies $\mathbf{a}_1$, which is close to the average
logit($q_x$) schedule, and so carries the **level**; the later columns
carry the **shape**.  Not all shape columns are equally useful.  The age
basis has $r_2$ = `{python} f"{r2}"` columns because the decomposition must
reproduce every observed schedule almost exactly (chapter 04), including the
year-to-year jitter of single-year rates in small populations.  That
jitter lives in the high-order columns.  Clustering on them groups
country-years by how noisy their data are, not by how their mortality
differs.

We therefore keep only the first $c_{\text{feat}}$ age columns, with $c_{\text{feat}}$ the smallest
number of age components that together capture a share
τ = `{python} f"{PF['column_share']}"` of the sum of squares of the observed
schedules — the τ rule that chapter 04 uses for $r_2$, at a
less demanding threshold (`mdmx.clustering.column_energy_share`,
`choose_n_columns`).  Two further measures, computed for every column,
show whether the rule separates signal from noise:

* the **roughness of the basis function** $\mathbf{a}_j$ — the sum of its
  squared second differences over ages 2–109 (`basis_roughness`): smooth
  age patterns have values near zero;
* the **year-to-year noise ratio** of each core entry (`year_to_year_noise`):
  the mean squared change between consecutive observed years of a
  population, divided by twice the entry's overall variance.  An entry
  that moves smoothly over time scores near 0; one whose values are
  independent from year to year scores near 1.


## Step 3 — level-free features

The features are built from the entries of columns
2–`{python} f"{k_cols}"` of $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}$, for both sex components:
the level column is dropped.  Dropping the level column is not enough to
make the remaining coordinates independent of level, because the *shape*
of the schedule changes systematically as mortality falls (the "rotation"
of the age pattern of decline; paper §4.1).  So, for each entry $m$, we
estimate its **e₀-conditional mean** $\mu_m(e_0)$ — a LOWESS curve of the
entry against e₀ fitted across all observed country-years, with a span of
`{python} f"{PF['conditional_frac']}"` of the points and
`{python} f"{PF['conditional_iterations']}"` robustifying iterations
(`mdmx.clustering.conditional_mean`) — and subtract it:

$$
f_{c,t,m} \;=\; \mathcal{G}_{c,t}[i_m, j_m] \;-\; \mu_m\!\left(e_{0,c,t}\right),
\qquad j_m \in \{2, \ldots, k\},\; i_m \in \{1, 2\}.
$$

The feature vector $\mathbf{f}_{c,t}$ (length
$2(k-1)$ = `{python} f"{2 * (k_cols - 1)}"`) says how this country-year's
schedule departs from the schedule that is typical at its e₀.  The same
LOWESS is applied to *every* entry of the effective core, including the
level column and the columns beyond $c_{\text{feat}}$; this **conditional-mean core**
$\bar{\mathcal{G}}(e_0)$ is stored on a grid of e₀ values (step
`{python} f"{PF['e0_grid_step']}"` years) so that later chapters can build
the typical schedule $\mathbf{S}\bar{\mathcal{G}}(e_0)\mathbf{A}^\top$ at any
e₀.

## Step 5 — Gaussian mixtures and the choice of K

A Gaussian mixture model (paper §4.2) treats the rows of the reduced
feature matrix as draws from $K$ multivariate normal distributions, each
with its own mean vector and its own unrestricted ("full") covariance
matrix.  It is fitted by EM for each $K$ = `{python} f"{PG['k_min']}"`, …,
`{python} f"{PG['k_max']}"`, with `{python} f"{PG['n_init']}"` EM starts
from k-means solutions (seed in the parameter file).  The usual choice of
$K$ minimises

$$
\mathrm{BIC}(K) = -2\,\ell(\hat\theta_K) + p_K \log n_{\text{obs}},
\qquad p_K = (K-1) + K d + K\,\tfrac{d(d+1)}{2}
$$

(`mdmx.clustering.bic_sweep` checks the formula against

BIC alone is not a sufficient guide: a partition is worth
describing only if the method gives it again, and it is more useful if it
would also appear in another set of populations.  We check both.

**Reproducibility.**  The likelihood of a mixture has many local maxima,
and EM started from other k-means solutions can end at a different
partition of almost the same likelihood.  For every $K$ the mixture is
refitted from `{python} f"{1 + len(PSt['seeds'])}"` seeds — the chapter's
and those listed under `seeds` in the parameter file, each with
`{python} f"{PG['n_init']}"` EM starts — and the partitions are compared in
pairs with the adjusted Rand index (ARI: 1 = identical, 0 = chance
agreement).  **The chapter uses the largest $K$ whose refits agree at a mean
ARI of at least `{python} f"{PSt['reproducibility_ari']}"`, as do the refits
of every smaller $K$** (`mdmx.clustering.choose_k_reproducible`); its
partition is that $K$'s fit from the sweep above.

**Resampling of populations.**  Reproducibility is a property of the
method on these data.  Whether the clusters would also appear in another
set of populations is a different question.  Following @Hennig2007, we
resample whole *population families* — a population together with its HMD
parts, such as the United Kingdom with England & Wales, Scotland and
Northern Ireland (`svdcomp.population_families`), because the years of one
population are not independent — with replacement, refit the mixture to
each of `{python} f"{PSt['bootstrap_resamples']}"` resamples
(each with the `{python} f"{PG['n_init']}"` EM starts of the chapter's
partition, so that the check measures the populations and not the
starting values), and record for
every cluster its Jaccard similarity with its best match in the refit, on
the country-years in the resample (`mdmx.clustering.group_bootstrap`,
`cluster_jaccard`).  A cluster's mean Jaccard is read as in Hennig's
`fpc::clusterboot`: at least `{python} f"{PSt['jaccard_stable']}"` a stable
cluster, from `{python} f"{PSt['jaccard_doubtful']}"` a pattern whose
members are doubtful, below that a cluster not to be trusted.  The check is
reported for every $K$; it does not choose $K$.

country-years after matching (ARI `{python} f"{ward_ari:.2f}"`).  The two
methods answer different questions — Ward minimises Euclidean within-group
spread, the mixture also models each cluster's own covariance — so their
agreement is only partial.

## Step 7 — what the clusters are

Each cluster is summarised by its **mean deviation** $\bar{\mathbf{f}}_k$, the
mean of its members' features (`mdmx.clustering.cluster_means`).  Mapped
back to core coordinates (`features_to_cores`: the feature entries filled
in, zeros elsewhere) it is a matrix $\mathbf{D}_k$, and the cluster's
schedule at any life expectancy $x$ is

$$
\mathcal{M}_k(x) \;=\; \mathbf{S}\,\bigl[\bar{\mathcal{G}}(x) + \mathbf{D}_k\bigr]\,\mathbf{A}^\top,
$$

the typical schedule at $x$ plus the cluster's deviation
$\mathbf{S}\mathbf{D}_k\mathbf{A}^\top$, which is the same at every $x$.
The members' own schedules, and their e₀, vary widely within a cluster;
what they share is the deviation.


## Step 8 — level, time, and noise

**Level and time.**  The features have no mean dependence on e₀, but the
clusters can still differ in e₀ if different groups of populations are
observed at different levels of mortality — below e₀ = 40 only a handful of
populations are in the HMD.  The correlation ratio $\eta^2$ (the share of a
variable's variance explained by cluster membership;
`mdmx.clustering.eta_squared`) measures this for e₀ and for calendar year;
the number of clusters present at each level of e₀ shows whether the
clusters are level strata.

Where the data allow a test, the clusters are not level strata: every
5-year e₀ band with at least 100 country-years contains at least
`{python} f"{int(clusters_per_band[well_filled].min())}"` clusters, and the

calendar year.  This is not level leaking back into the features, whose
mean does not depend on e₀ (Step 3).  What association there is comes from
composition — below e₀ ≈ 45 only a few populations with long early series
are observed, so the deviation patterns found there are theirs, and
populations that enter the HMD late are observed only at high e₀ — and from
spread: the residuals are larger at high mortality, and clusters that
differ mainly in how widely their members scatter (see Step 7) differ in

sense intended — they are defined by deviations from the typical schedule
at the same e₀ — but they are not uncorrelated with level or period.

populations, above all when the features include the high-order age

## Step 9 — countries, regions and periods

### Majority labels

Two derived clusterings, in the companion only, summarise the country-year labels: a
population's **country cluster** is the cluster of the majority of its observed
country-years, and a year's **year cluster** is the cluster of the majority of
populations observed that year.  Both come from `mdmx.clustering.majority_label`, the
single implementation of every majority vote in this chapter: only observed
country-years vote, a population or year with none gets no label (`-1`),

# Part B — Level epochs

## The level signal and its direction

The level coordinate $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1]$ removed in Part A is used
here to describe *how fast mortality is changing* (the level epochs, which are in the
companion only).  Its sign
is that of the singular vectors $\mathbf{s}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_1$, which is a
convention: in this decomposition $\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1]$ *falls* as e₀
rises, but another linear-algebra library or sign rule could flip it.  To
make "improvement" mean improvement whatever the convention, we multiply
the level coordinate by the sign of its correlation with e₀
(`mdmx.clustering.level_orientation`):

$$
\ell_{c,t} = \sigma\, \mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1], \qquad
\sigma = \operatorname{sign}\operatorname{corr}\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{c,t}[1,1],\, e_0\bigr),
$$

so that $\ell$ rises when mortality improves whatever the orientation of the
bases (with the decomposition chapter's orientation, $\sigma = +1$).  The
thresholds below are stated for $\ell$.  To express them in years of life expectancy, we regress e₀ on
$\ell$ across all observed country-years: one unit of $\ell$ corresponds to
$b$ years of e₀.

```{python}
#| label: c06-orientation
#| code-summary: "Orient the level signal by e0 and translate the thresholds into e0 units"

ell = sigma * level
e0_per_ell, e0_at_zero = np.polyfit(ell, e0, 1)
print(f"σ = {sigma:+d}: the level coordinate G[1,1] {'falls' if sigma < 0 else 'rises'} as e0 rises")
```

`{python} f"{PE['delta_rapid'] * e0_per_ell:.2f}"` years of e₀ per year
(two years per decade).

## The classification

The procedure takes every setting from the parameter file:

1. **Smooth.**  For each population with at least
   `{python} f"{PE['min_years']}"` observed years, smooth $\ell$ and e₀
   over its observed years by LOWESS (`mdmx.clustering.lowess_at`), with a
   span of `{python} f"{PE['lowess_frac']}"` of the population's years but
   never fewer than `{python} f"{PE['lowess_min_points']}"` years, and
   `{python} f"{PE['lowess_iterations']}"` robustness iterations.
2. **Slopes.**  Slide a window of $W$ = `{python} f"{PE['window']}"`
   calendar years across the series one year at a time; in each window
   with at least `{python} f"{PE['min_points']}"` observed years, fit a
   least-squares line to the smoothed $\ell$ and keep its slope
   (`mdmx.clustering.window_slopes`).
3. **Classify** each window slope $s$: rapid improvement if
   $s > \delta_{\text{rapid}}$, slow improvement if
   $\delta < s \le \delta_{\text{rapid}}$, stagnation if $|s| \le \delta$,
   slow worsening if $-\delta_{\text{rapid}} \le s < -\delta$, rapid
   worsening if $s < -\delta_{\text{rapid}}$ (`classify_slopes`).
4. **Assign years.**  Each calendar year from the population's first to
   last observed year takes the most common category among the windows
   that contain it (`yearly_categories`, which uses `majority_label`);
   ties go to the category listed first above, i.e. towards rapid
   improvement.  Years in the middle of a series are covered by $W$
   windows; years near either end by fewer, so their category rests on
   less evidence.
5. **Epochs** are maximal runs of consecutive calendar years with the
   same category (`runs`).

# The `clustering` artefact

Everything later chapters need is saved as one artefact, `clustering`.
Per-observation arrays have one row per observed country-year in the order
of `c_idx`, `t_idx` (by population, then year).  Age columns of the core
are 0-based in the arrays (column 0 is the level).

| Key | Shape / type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| `c_idx`, `t_idx` | (n_obs,) int | population and year index of each observed country-year |
| `pops`, `years` | (C,), (T,) | population codes and calendar years of those indices |
| `e0` | (n_obs, S) | e₀ of the observed schedule (female, male); `e0_mean` (n_obs,) is their average |
| `K`, `labels`, `posterior` | int, (n_obs,), (n_obs, K) | clusters 0 … K−1 (figures and paper: 1 … K), numbered by mean e₀; K is the reproducible K of Step 5 |
| `K_bic` | int | the K with the lowest BIC (not used for the partition) |
| `noise_clusters` | (m,) int | clusters flagged as small-population noise clusters (Step 8); exclude them when using the clusters |
| `noise_measures` | dict | `excess_log_roughness` (n_obs,) log₁₀ roughness minus its e₀-conditional mean, `small` (n_obs,) bool, in the smallest quarter by population size (Step 8) |
| `stability` | dict | `reproducibility` (ARI between refits, by K), `bootstrap_by_k` (Jaccard and ARI under resampling of population families, by K), `jaccard` (K,) each cluster's mean Jaccard, `jaccard_present` (K,) resamples holding the cluster |
| `features` | dict | `n_columns` (k), `index` (p, 2) = (sex component, age column) of each feature, `raw` (n_obs, p) core entries, `values` (n_obs, p) level-free features |
| `conditional_mean` | dict | `e0_grid` (g,), `core` (g, r₁, r₂) e₀-conditional mean of the full effective core, `features` (g, p) the same at the feature positions, `frac`, `iterations` |
| `residual_mean` | (K, p) | each cluster's mean feature vector |
| `residual_core` | (K, r₁, r₂) | the same in core coordinates (zero outside the feature positions) |
| `deviation_logit` | (K, S, A) | the cluster's deviation $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{D}_k\mathbf{A}^\top$ in logit(q_x) |
| `member_mean_core`, `member_mean_e0` | (K, r₁, r₂), (K,) | mean effective core and mean e₀ of the members |
| `pca`, `scores` | dict, (n_obs, d) | `mean`, `components` (p, d), `explained_variance_ratio`, `d`; the reduced features |
| `gmm` | dict | `weights`, `means`, `covariances`, `covariance_type` (in PCA coordinates) |
| `bic` | DataFrame | BIC/AIC for every K |
| `ward_labels`, `ward_agreement`, `ward_ari` | | Ward groups (matched to clusters) and agreement |
| `country_cluster`, `year_cluster` | (C,), (T,) int | majority labels (−1: no observed country-years) |
| `level_sign`, `e0_per_level` | int, float | σ and b of Part B |
| `epochs`, `epoch_years`, `epoch_windows` | DataFrames | epoch table, per-year categories, window slopes |

To build the schedule of cluster `k` at a target life expectancy `x`:

```python
core = clus.conditional_core_at(x, art["conditional_mean"]["e0_grid"], art["conditional_mean"]["core"])
core = core + art["residual_core"][k]                       # (r1, r2)
logit_qx = tz.schedules_from_cores(core, tucker["U_sex"], tucker["U_age"])   # (S, A)
```

Its e₀ is close to, but not exactly, `x` (Step 3); adjust the level
coordinate `core[0, 0]` if an exact e₀ is required.  A new schedule (e.g. a
fitted one) is placed in the clusters' feature space with
`clus.cores_to_features(G, index) − clus.cores_to_features(conditional_core_at(e0, …), index)`
followed by `clus.pca_transform(…, art["pca"])`.

age columns $c_{\text{feat}}$, with other LOWESS spans for the conditional mean, and with

```{python}
def _variant_features(n_columns, frac, standardise):
    """Level-free features for one variant of the specification."""
    idx_v = clus.feature_index(r1, n_columns)
    raw_v = clus.cores_to_features(G_obs, idx_v)
    cm_v = {**cm, "frac": frac}
    fit_v, _ = clus.conditional_mean(e0, raw_v, **cm_v)
    res_v = raw_v - fit_v
    if standardise:
        scale2, _ = clus.conditional_mean(e0, res_v ** 2, **cm_v)
        res_v = res_v / np.sqrt(np.clip(scale2, 1e-12, None))
    return res_v

def _rule_k(X_v, cov):
    """K of the agreed rule (Step 5) for features X_v, and the refits' mean ARI at it."""

                for cov in PS["sensitivity_covariance"]]

                 for k_ in PS["sensitivity_columns"]]
    variants += [(f"LOWESS span {f_}", k_cols, f_, False, PG["covariance_type"])
                 for f_ in PS["sensitivity_frac"]]
    if PS["sensitivity_standardised"]:
        variants.append(("standardised residuals", k_cols, PF["conditional_frac"], True, PG["covariance_type"]))
    variants.append(("no pooling", k_cols, PF["conditional_frac"], False, PG["covariance_type"]))

        res_v = _nopool_features() if name == "no pooling" else _variant_features(k_, f_, std)
        X_v = clus.pca_fit(res_v, PF["pca_variance"])["scores"]
```
