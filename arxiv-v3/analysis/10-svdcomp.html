13.1 The task

The previous chapters build mortality schedules from the decomposition. This chapter addresses a different, very practical question (paper §8): given only a summary indicator of mortality, what is the most likely complete schedule? In much of the world, child mortality ₅q₀ — the probability of dying before age 5 — is the only well-measured indicator, sometimes together with adult mortality ₄₅q₁₅, the probability that a 15-year-old dies before age 60. Model life-table systems turn such indicators into full life tables; the best known are Coale–Demeny, the Log-Quad model of Wilmoth et al. (2012) and SVD-Comp (Clark 2019).

MDMx reformulates SVD-Comp in the Tucker framework. Every schedule — the \(2 \times 110\) matrix of \(\operatorname{logit}(q_x)\) for one country-year — is written in the shared sex basis \(\mathbf{S}\) and age basis \(\mathbf{A}\) of chapter 04 as \(\mathcal{M}_{c,t} = \mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\,\mathbf{A}^\top\). Stacking the female and then the male schedule into one vector \(\mathbf{z}\) of length \(2A = 220\), the reconstruction is linear in the core,

\[ \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{R}\,\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal{G}), \qquad \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{S} \otimes \mathbf{A}_{c} \qquad\text{(paper Eq. 21)}, \]

where \(\mathbf{A}_{c}\) keeps only the first \(c_{\text{age}}\) columns of the age basis. A small neural network \(f_\theta\) maps the logit of the input indicators to the \(2 c_{\text{age}}\) core weights (paper Eq. 22), trained so that \(\mathbf{R} f_\theta(\cdot)\) — the whole schedule — matches the observed schedule, with extra weight on ages 0–4. Finally, the predicted core weights are moved by (to first order) the smallest amount that makes the schedule reproduce its inputs exactly, as Log-Quad does (paper §8.3, Eq. 23). Two models are fitted (§8.2):

  • Model 1 — inputs \(\operatorname{logit}({}_5q_0^F)\) and \(\operatorname{logit}({}_5q_0^M)\);
  • Model 2 — additionally \(\operatorname{logit}({}_{45}q_{15}^F)\) and \(\operatorname{logit}({}_{45}q_{15}^M)\).

Both predict both sexes at once, so the sex differential comes from one model in one basis.

13.2 Step 1 — the training data: observed schedules and their indicators

The models are trained on every observed (non-exceptional) country-year of the tensor. For each we take the observed \(\operatorname{logit}(q_x)\) schedule of both sexes and compute its ₅q₀ and ₄₅q₁₅ with the project’s single life-table implementation (mdmx.lifetable.nqx). The indicators are therefore exactly consistent with the schedules the models must reproduce.

13.3 Step 2 — the truncated basis, and what truncation alone costs

The network predicts only the first \(c_{\text{age}}\) age components of the \(r_2\) = 50 in the basis. Two arguments favour a small \(c_{\text{age}}\) such as 6 (12 core weights): the higher components describe fine-grained structure that one or two summary indicators cannot be expected to predict, and the first few capture nearly all of the age-specific variance. Both arguments can be checked. For these checks the chapter uses fixed settings, \(c_{\text{age}}\) = 6 and a weight \(w_{\mathrm{child}}\) = 10 on ages 0–4 in the loss (Step 3); the final models’ settings are chosen by nested cross-validation (Step 8).

The second argument depends on what “variance” means. An oracle helps. For every observed schedule, take the core weights that reproduce it as closely as possible in the truncated basis — the least-squares projection (mdmx.svdcomp.truncation_oracle). No predictor of truncated core weights can come closer to the schedule in squared error, so the oracle’s squared error is the price of the truncation alone (in \(e_0\) it is a reference, not a bound). We measure it three ways: the share of the uncentred sum of squares of logit(q_x) captured (what a variance threshold on the decomposition measures, dominated by the overall level of mortality); the share of the variation around the mean schedule captured; and, most interpretably, the error in life expectancy.

The oracle also reveals a side-effect of the loss. The loss of Step 3 up-weights ages 0–4 by \(w_{\mathrm{child}}\); with weights, the best truncated core is the weighted least-squares fit, which buys a closer fit at ages 0–4 at the expense of every other age. We therefore compute the oracle both without weights (\(w_{\mathrm{child}} = 0\)) and with the fixed setting \(w_{\mathrm{child}}\) = 10.0.

13.4 Step 3 — the network and its training

The network (mdmx.nn.MLP) has the paper’s architecture: inputs → two hidden layers of 64 ReLU units → a linear output of \(2 c_{\text{age}}\) values. Three design choices make it train reliably:

  • Inputs are standardised (mdmx.nn.Standardizer) to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, using the training rows only.
  • Outputs are standardised core weights. The core weights have very different scales: across the observed schedules the first averages 73 (standard deviation 12), whereas the 6th varies around zero with a standard deviation of 0.76. The network predicts each weight in standard-deviation units, and the fixed affine map back to core weights is folded into the reconstruction: \(\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{R}\,(\text{out} \odot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \boldsymbol{\mu})\).
  • The loss is measured on the schedule (mdmx.nn.LinearDecoderLoss): the weighted squared error between \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) and the observed \(\mathbf{z}\), with every cell weighted \(1/(2A)\) and the ten cells at ages 0–4 weighted an extra \(w_{\mathrm{child}}/10\) (the loss of the article’s §8.2).

Training (mdmx.nn.fit_mlp) is mini-batch Adam with weight decay, and early stopping: 10% of the training rows — whole population families, so that validation also measures prediction for unseen populations — are held out, the weights of the epoch with the lowest validation loss are kept, and training stops after 30 epochs without improvement. mdmx.nn is checked by unit tests (tests/test_nn.py): the hand-written gradients agree with finite differences, and training is reproducible from its seed.

13.5 Step 4 — making the predictions reproduce their inputs

A network trained to minimise a reconstruction loss does not reproduce its inputs exactly: the schedule it predicts for ₅q₀ = 0.05 implies a ₅q₀ near, but not equal to, 0.05. A user who supplies ₅q₀ and ₄₅q₁₅ expects them back — Log-Quad guarantees it by construction — and errors in the indicators propagate into life expectancy. MDMx therefore ends with a correction step (mdmx.svdcomp.enforce_indicators).

Let \(\mathbf{g}_0\) be the network’s core weights for one country-year and \(t_1, \dots, t_m\) its input indicators (\(m = 2\) for Model 1: ₅q₀ of each sex; \(m = 4\) for Model 2: also ₄₅q₁₅ of each sex). For a schedule \(\mathbf{z}\), write \(h_j(\mathbf{z}) = 1 - \prod_{a \in W_j} \bigl(1 - \operatorname{expit}(z_a)\bigr)\) for the indicator over the ages \(W_j\) of one sex (ages 0–4, or 15–59), and \(f_j(\mathbf{g}) = \operatorname{logit} h_j(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}) - \operatorname{logit} t_j\) for its error. Since \(\partial \operatorname{logit} h_j / \partial z_a = q_a / h_j\) for ages \(a\) in \(W_j\) (and 0 elsewhere), the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J} = \partial \mathbf{f} / \partial \mathbf{g}\) (\(m \times 2c_{\text{age}}\)) is cheap to compute.

The directions. To first order, the smallest change of core weights that removes the errors is \(\Delta\mathbf{g} = -\mathbf{J}^\top(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g}_0)\), a combination of the \(m\) columns of \(\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{g}_0)^\top\). Column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{D}\) is the gradient of indicator \(j\) with respect to the schedule, projected onto the truncated basis: the change of schedule that moves indicator \(j\) furthest per unit of change, concentrated in that indicator’s age window as far as the basis allows.

The coefficients. The corrected core weights are

\[ \mathbf{g}^\star = \mathbf{g}_0 + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mu}, \qquad\text{with } \boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^m \text{ solving } \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g}_0 + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \mathbf{0}, \]

\(m\) equations in \(m\) unknowns, solved by Newton’s method from \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}\), \(\boldsymbol{\mu} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\mu} - \bigl(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{g})\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})\), halving a step whenever it would not reduce the largest error. The predicted schedule is \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}^\star\). Three properties make this the natural choice:

  • It stays inside the MDMx representation. The correction is a change of core weights, so the corrected schedule is still a combination of the shared sex and age basis functions — smooth across age, and built from the same components for both sexes.
  • It is (to first order) the smallest possible change to the predicted schedule. The columns of \(\mathbf{R}\) are orthonormal, so \(\lVert \mathbf{g} - \mathbf{g}_0 \rVert\) is exactly the root-sum-of-squares change of the whole logit(q_x) schedule; the directions \(\mathbf{D}\) are those of the smallest change, and the coefficients make the indicators exact rather than approximately right. The predicted age pattern is altered as little as the constraints allow, and only where it must be. (Re-computing the directions at every step would converge to the exactly smallest change, but that iteration can fail when the start is far from the targets; tests/test_svdcomp.py checks that our change is within 5% of the exactly smallest one, and in practice it is within about 1%.)
  • It respects the sexes. The span of \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{S} \otimes \mathbf{A}_c\) is “any female schedule in the span of \(\mathbf{A}_c\), and any male schedule in the span of \(\mathbf{A}_c\)”, so the direction of a female indicator alters only the female schedule, and both sexes are corrected in the same age basis.

The directions are fixed at \(\mathbf{g}_0\), so each Newton step solves an \(m \times m\) system per country-year: the correction is vectorised over country-years and takes well under a second for all 4,056 of them. Iteration stops when every \(|\operatorname{logit} h_j - \operatorname{logit} t_j|\) is below 1e-10; a country-year that has not converged after 50 iterations is counted and reported (Step 8). tests/test_svdcomp.py also checks that the inputs are reproduced to machine precision and that a female-only constraint leaves the male schedule untouched.

What does the correction look like? It is a combination of the directions \(\mathbf{D}\): the figure shows, for the mean schedule, the smallest change that raises logit ₅q₀ by 0.1 while keeping ₄₅q₁₅ fixed, and the smallest change that raises logit ₄₅q₁₅ by 0.1 while keeping ₅q₀ fixed. With six components the change is smooth and spreads somewhat beyond the age window (a bump confined to ages 0–4 cannot be built from six smooth curves); with more components it becomes more local. 13.6 Step 5 — cross-validation blocked by population family

An honest estimate of how well a model predicts a schedule it has not seen must hold out data that are genuinely new. Neighbouring years of the same population are nearly identical, so holding out years at random tests interpolation, not prediction. We hold out whole populations instead — and more than that, whole population families: some HMD series overlap (the United Kingdom contains England & Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland; Germany contains East and West Germany; New Zealand contains its Māori and non-Māori populations), and a model trained on England & Wales has effectively seen the United Kingdom. A family is a population plus every population it contains, read from the population register.

The 41 families are dealt into 10 folds, largest first, each to the fold with the fewest country-years so far (mdmx.svdcomp.assign_folds). Every model — MDMx and the benchmarks — is trained on 9 folds and predicts the remaining one, 10 times; each country-year’s prediction therefore comes from a model that has never seen its population.

13.8 Step 7 — how many age components, and how much weight on ages 0–4?

13.9 Step 8 — choosing the settings honestly: nested cross-validation

If \(c_{\text{age}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{child}}\) were chosen as the best point of Step 7 and that point’s error quoted, the test folds would have helped choose the model that is then judged on them. A nested cross-validation avoids this (mdmx.svdcomp.nested_cross_validate_core_model). For each of the 10 outer folds:

  1. the population families of the nine training folds are dealt into 3 inner folds;
  2. every candidate \((c_{\text{age}}, w_{\mathrm{child}})\) in [6, 20] × [0.0, 10.0] is cross-validated over those inner folds, on training rows only, and scored by the mean (over both sexes) absolute error of \(e_0\) of its corrected predictions;
  3. the best candidate is trained on all training rows of the outer fold and predicts the held-out fold.

The grid and the number of inner folds are kept small (four candidates, three inner folds: about 260 networks); the sweep of Step 7 shows that the omitted values of \(c_{\text{age}}\) would not change the picture. The sex-specific variant used in Step 9 is not tuned separately: in each outer fold it uses the setting chosen for the corresponding two-sex model, a choice that was also made without the test fold.

13.10 Step 9 — benchmarks: Log-Quad and SVD-Comp on the same folds

Comparing MDMx fairly with Log-Quad and SVD-Comp needs three things: the benchmarks must be fitted to the same training data, evaluated on the same held-out country-years, and given the same information.

  • Log-Quad (Wilmoth et al. (2012); mdmx.logquad) models the log death rate in each five-year age group as a quadratic in \(\log {}_5q_0\) plus an adult-mortality term \(v_x k\). It is re-estimated on each fold’s training schedules: \(a_x, b_x, c_x\) by least squares age group by age group, and \(v_x\) as the leading singular vector of the residuals at ages 5–89. With ₅q₀ only, \(k = 0\); with ₄₅q₁₅, \(k\) is solved so that the predicted ₄₅q₁₅ equals the input exactly. ₅q₀ is always reproduced exactly.
  • SVD-Comp [Clark (2019); mdmx.svdcomp.fit_svdcomp_classic] takes the SVD of each sex’s logit(q_x) matrix, keeps four components, and predicts their weights by regressions on eight polynomial terms in ₅q₀ and ₄₅q₁₅; with ₅q₀ only, ₄₅q₁₅ is first predicted from ₅q₀. It too is re-estimated on each fold’s training schedules, and, as published, it reproduces its inputs only approximately.

Using the published coefficients instead would be unfair to MDMx in the opposite direction: both were fitted to HMD life tables that include every population we test on. Re-estimation is possible because the model forms are fully specified; the re-implementations were checked during development against the originals: the SVD-Comp prediction code reproduces the R package svdComp5q0 to \(10^{-14}\) when given its packaged coefficients, and Log-Quad fitted to all observed HMD schedules recovers the published coefficients closely. tests/test_svdcomp.py checks that each benchmark recovers data generated by its own model.

The information difference. Log-Quad and SVD-Comp predict each sex from that sex’s indicators only. MDMx Models 1 and 2 use both sexes’ indicators — Model 1 sees the male ₅q₀ when predicting females. That is a legitimate feature of a joint model, but it makes a straight comparison unfair to the benchmarks. We therefore also report a sex-specific MDMx variant: one network per sex whose only inputs are that sex’s indicators, whose loss covers only that sex’s schedule, and whose predictions are corrected to reproduce those inputs. The table also shows MDMx at the fixed settings of Step 6.

All methods are compared on two scales: \(e_0\), and the RMSE of \(\operatorname{logit}({}_nq_x)\) over the standard abridged age groups (0, 1–4, 5–9, …, 105–109). The abridged scale is the one on which Log-Quad is defined, so it does not penalise Log-Quad for spreading each group’s rate evenly over single years; the single-year models are aggregated to the same groups.

13.11 Step 10 — the bases within each fold

The sex and age bases \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\) of chapter 04 come from every observed schedule, those of any held-out fold included. A cross-validation in those bases has a small leak: the bases have “seen” the test populations, whereas Log-Quad and SVD-Comp are estimated on the training folds alone. Every cross-validation of this chapter therefore re-estimates the bases from the training rows of each fit — the SVD of those schedules, then the same smoothing and re-orthonormalisation as in chapter 04, with the age rank held at its value on all the data. ## The task

The previous chapters build mortality schedules from the decomposition.
This chapter addresses a different, very practical question (paper §8):
*given only a summary indicator of mortality, what is the most likely
complete schedule?*  In much of the world, child mortality ₅q₀ — the
probability of dying before age 5 — is the only well-measured indicator,
sometimes together with adult mortality ₄₅q₁₅, the probability that a
15-year-old dies before age 60.  Model life-table systems turn such
indicators into full life tables; the best known are Coale–Demeny, the
Log-Quad model of @Wilmoth2012 and SVD-Comp [@Clark2019].

MDMx reformulates SVD-Comp in the Tucker framework.  Every schedule — the
$2 \times 110$ matrix of $\operatorname{logit}(q_x)$ for one country-year —
is written in the shared sex basis $\mathbf{S}$ and age basis $\mathbf{A}$
of chapter 04 as $\mathcal{M}_{c,t} = \mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\,\mathbf{A}^\top$.
Stacking the female and then the male schedule into one vector
$\mathbf{z}$ of length $2A = 220$, the reconstruction is linear in the
core,

$$
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{R}\,\operatorname{vec}(\mathcal{G}), \qquad
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{S} \otimes \mathbf{A}_{c}
\qquad\text{(paper Eq. 21)},
$$

where $\mathbf{A}_{c}$ keeps only the first $c_{\text{age}}$ columns of the
age basis.  A small neural network $f_\theta$ maps the logit of the input
indicators to the $2 c_{\text{age}}$ core weights (paper Eq. 22), trained
so that $\mathbf{R} f_\theta(\cdot)$ — the *whole schedule* — matches the
observed schedule, with extra weight on ages 0–4.
Finally, the predicted core weights are moved by (to first order) the
smallest amount that makes the schedule reproduce its inputs *exactly*, as
Log-Quad does (paper §8.3, Eq. 23).  Two models are fitted (§8.2):

* **Model 1** — inputs $\operatorname{logit}({}_5q_0^F)$ and
  $\operatorname{logit}({}_5q_0^M)$;
* **Model 2** — additionally $\operatorname{logit}({}_{45}q_{15}^F)$ and
  $\operatorname{logit}({}_{45}q_{15}^M)$.

Both predict both sexes at once, so the sex differential comes from one
model in one basis.

single life-table implementation (`mdmx.lifetable.nqx`).  The indicators
are therefore exactly consistent with the schedules the models must

## Step 2 — the truncated basis, and what truncation alone costs

The network predicts only the first $c_{\text{age}}$ age components of
the $r_2$ = `{python} U_age.shape[1]` in the basis.  Two arguments favour
a small $c_{\text{age}}$ such as `{python} PS["c_age"]`
(`{python} 2 * PS["c_age"]` core weights): the higher components describe
fine-grained structure that one or two summary indicators cannot be
expected to predict, and the first few capture nearly all of the
age-specific variance.  Both arguments can be checked.  For these checks
the chapter uses **fixed settings**, $c_{\text{age}}$ = `{python} PS["c_age"]`
and a weight $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ = `{python} f"{PS['alpha']:g}"` on ages 0–4 in the
loss (Step 3); the final models' settings are chosen by nested
cross-validation (Step 8).

The second argument depends on what "variance" means.  An **oracle** helps.
For every observed schedule, take the core weights that reproduce it as
closely as possible in the truncated basis — the least-squares projection
(`mdmx.svdcomp.truncation_oracle`).  No predictor of truncated core weights
can come closer to the schedule in squared error, so the oracle's squared
error is the price of the truncation alone (in $e_0$ it is a reference,
not a bound).  We measure it three ways: the share of the *uncentred*
sum of squares of logit(q_x) captured (what a variance threshold on the
decomposition measures, dominated by the overall level of mortality); the
share of the variation *around the mean schedule* captured; and, most
interpretably, the error in life expectancy.

The oracle also reveals a side-effect of the loss.  The loss of Step 3
up-weights ages 0–4 by $w_{\mathrm{child}}$; with weights, the best truncated core is
the *weighted* least-squares fit, which buys a closer fit at ages 0–4 at
the expense of every other age.  We therefore compute the oracle both
without weights ($w_{\mathrm{child}} = 0$) and with the fixed setting $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ =
`{python} PS["alpha"]`.

## Step 3 — the network and its training

The network (`mdmx.nn.MLP`) has the paper's architecture: inputs → two
hidden layers of `{python} PS["hidden"][0]` ReLU units → a linear output of
$2 c_{\text{age}}$ values.  Three design choices make it train reliably:

* **Inputs are standardised** (`mdmx.nn.Standardizer`) to mean 0 and
  standard deviation 1, using the training rows only.
* **Outputs are standardised core weights.**  The core weights have very
  different scales: across the observed schedules the first averages
  `{python} f"{cores_obs[:, 0].mean():.0f}"` (standard deviation
  `{python} f"{cores_obs[:, 0].std():.0f}"`), whereas the
  `{python} PS["c_age"]`th varies around zero with a standard deviation of
  `{python} f"{cores_obs[:, PS['c_age'] - 1].std():.2f}"`.  The network
  predicts each weight in standard-deviation units, and the fixed affine
  map back to core weights is folded into the reconstruction:
  $\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{R}\,(\text{out} \odot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
* **The loss is measured on the schedule** (`mdmx.nn.LinearDecoderLoss`):
  the weighted squared error between $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ and the observed
  $\mathbf{z}$, with every cell weighted $1/(2A)$ and the ten cells at ages
  0–4 weighted an extra $w_{\mathrm{child}}/10$ (the loss of the article's §8.2).

Training (`mdmx.nn.fit_mlp`) is mini-batch Adam with weight decay, and
early stopping: `{python} f"{100 * PS['val_frac']:.0f}"`% of the training
rows — whole population families, so that validation also measures
prediction for unseen populations — are held out, the weights of the
epoch with the lowest validation loss are kept, and training stops after
`{python} PS["patience"]` epochs without improvement.  `mdmx.nn` is
checked by unit tests (`tests/test_nn.py`): the hand-written gradients agree
with finite differences, and training is reproducible from its seed.

## Step 4 — making the predictions reproduce their inputs {#sec-c10-enforce}

A network trained to minimise a reconstruction loss does not reproduce its
inputs exactly: the schedule it predicts for ₅q₀ = 0.05 implies a ₅q₀ near,
but not equal to, 0.05.  A user who supplies ₅q₀ and ₄₅q₁₅ expects them
back — Log-Quad guarantees it by construction — and errors in the
indicators propagate into life expectancy.  MDMx therefore ends with a
correction step (`mdmx.svdcomp.enforce_indicators`).

Let $\mathbf{g}_0$ be the network's core weights for one country-year and
$t_1, \dots, t_m$ its input indicators ($m = 2$ for Model 1: ₅q₀ of each
sex; $m = 4$ for Model 2: also ₄₅q₁₅ of each sex).  For a schedule
$\mathbf{z}$, write $h_j(\mathbf{z}) = 1 - \prod_{a \in W_j} \bigl(1 -
\operatorname{expit}(z_a)\bigr)$ for the indicator over the ages $W_j$ of
one sex (ages 0–4, or 15–59), and
$f_j(\mathbf{g}) = \operatorname{logit} h_j(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}) - \operatorname{logit} t_j$
for its error.  Since $\partial \operatorname{logit} h_j / \partial z_a =
q_a / h_j$ for ages $a$ in $W_j$ (and 0 elsewhere), the Jacobian
$\mathbf{J} = \partial \mathbf{f} / \partial \mathbf{g}$ ($m \times 2c_{\text{age}}$)
is cheap to compute.

**The directions.**  To first order, the smallest change of core weights
that removes the errors is
$\Delta\mathbf{g} = -\mathbf{J}^\top(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g}_0)$,
a combination of the $m$ columns of $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{g}_0)^\top$.
Column $j$ of $\mathbf{D}$ is the gradient of indicator $j$ with respect to
the schedule, projected onto the truncated basis: the change of schedule
that moves indicator $j$ furthest per unit of change, concentrated in that
indicator's age window as far as the basis allows.

**The coefficients.**  The corrected core weights are

$$
\mathbf{g}^\star = \mathbf{g}_0 + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mu},
\qquad\text{with } \boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^m \text{ solving }
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g}_0 + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \mathbf{0},
$$

$m$ equations in $m$ unknowns, solved by Newton's method from
$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}$,
$\boldsymbol{\mu} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\mu} - \bigl(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{g})\,\mathbf{D}\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g})$,
halving a step whenever it would not reduce the largest error.  The
predicted schedule is $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}^\star$.  Three properties make
this the natural choice:

* **It stays inside the MDMx representation.**  The correction is a change
  of core weights, so the corrected schedule is still a combination of the
  shared sex and age basis functions — smooth across age, and built from
  the same components for both sexes.
* **It is (to first order) the smallest possible change to the predicted
  schedule.**  The columns of $\mathbf{R}$ are orthonormal, so
  $\lVert \mathbf{g} - \mathbf{g}_0 \rVert$ is exactly the root-sum-of-squares
  change of the whole logit(q_x) schedule; the directions $\mathbf{D}$ are
  those of the smallest change, and the coefficients make the indicators
  exact rather than approximately right.  The predicted age pattern is
  altered as little as the constraints allow, and only where it must be.
  (Re-computing the directions at every step would converge to the
  exactly smallest change, but that iteration can fail when the start is
  far from the targets; `tests/test_svdcomp.py` checks that our change is
  within 5% of the exactly smallest one, and in practice it is within
  about 1%.)
* **It respects the sexes.**  The span of $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{S} \otimes
  \mathbf{A}_c$ is "any female schedule in the span of $\mathbf{A}_c$,
  and any male schedule in the span of $\mathbf{A}_c$", so the direction
  of a female indicator alters only the female schedule, and both sexes
  are corrected in the same age basis.

The directions are fixed at $\mathbf{g}_0$, so each Newton step solves an
$m \times m$ system per country-year: the correction is vectorised over
country-years and takes well under a second for all
`{python} f"{n_obs:,}"` of them.  Iteration stops when every
$|\operatorname{logit} h_j - \operatorname{logit} t_j|$ is below
`{python} f"{PS['enforce_tol']:.0e}"`; a country-year that has not
converged after `{python} PS["enforce_max_iter"]` iterations is counted
and reported (Step 8).  `tests/test_svdcomp.py` also checks that the
inputs are reproduced to machine precision and that a female-only
constraint leaves the male schedule untouched.

## Step 5 — cross-validation blocked by population family {#sec-c10-folds}

An honest estimate of how well a model predicts a schedule it has not seen
must hold out data that are genuinely new.  Neighbouring years of the same
population are nearly identical, so holding out *years* at random tests
interpolation, not prediction.  We hold out whole
populations instead — and more than that, whole **population families**:
some HMD series overlap (the United Kingdom contains England & Wales,
Scotland and Northern Ireland; Germany contains East and West Germany;
New Zealand contains its Māori and non-Māori populations), and a model
trained on England & Wales has effectively seen the United Kingdom.  A
family is a population plus every population it contains, read from the
population register.

The `{python} len(np.unique(family))` families are dealt into
`{python} PS["n_folds"]` folds, largest first, each to the fold with the
fewest country-years so far (`mdmx.svdcomp.assign_folds`).  Every model —
MDMx and the benchmarks — is trained on `{python} PS["n_folds"] - 1` folds
and predicts the remaining one, `{python} PS["n_folds"]` times; each
country-year's prediction therefore comes from a model that has never seen
its population.

## Step 7 — how many age components, and how much weight on ages 0–4? {#sec-c10-cage}

With all $r_2$ age components the predicted schedules could be noisy,
since the higher-order weights can hardly be predicted from one or two
summary indicators (Step 2); the weight $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ on ages 0–4 trades
accuracy at those ages against accuracy elsewhere.  We repeat the
whole cross-validation for $c_{\text{age}} \in$ `{python} PS["c_age_grid"]`
and $w_{\mathrm{child}} \in$ `{python} PS["alpha_grid"]`, for both models, recording
the accuracy with and without the correction.  This describes the
trade-offs; it is *not* how the settings are chosen (that is Step 8),
because choosing by these out-of-fold errors and then quoting them would
be optimistic.

## Step 8 — choosing the settings honestly: nested cross-validation {#sec-c10-nested}

If $c_{\text{age}}$ and $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ were chosen as the best point of Step 7
and that point's error quoted, the test folds would have helped choose the
model that is then judged on them.  A **nested cross-validation** avoids
this (`mdmx.svdcomp.nested_cross_validate_core_model`).  For each of the
`{python} PS["n_folds"]` outer folds:

1. the population families of the nine *training* folds are dealt into
   `{python} PS["nested_inner_folds"]` inner folds;
2. every candidate $(c_{\text{age}}, w_{\mathrm{child}})$ in
   `{python} PS["nested_c_age_grid"]` × `{python} PS["nested_alpha_grid"]`
   is cross-validated over those inner folds, on training rows only, and
   scored by the mean (over both sexes) absolute error of $e_0$ of its
   corrected predictions;
3. the best candidate is trained on all training rows of the outer fold
   and predicts the held-out fold.

The grid and the number of inner folds are kept small (four candidates,
three inner folds: about 260 networks); the sweep of Step 7 shows that the omitted
values of $c_{\text{age}}$ would not change the picture.  The sex-specific
variant used in Step 9 is not tuned separately: in each outer fold it uses
the setting chosen for the corresponding two-sex model, a choice that was
also made without the test fold.

```{python}
#| label: c10-nested
#| code-summary: "Nested cross-validation of Models 1 and 2, and the sex-specific variant (cached; the longest step)"
GRID = [{"c_age": int(c), "alpha": float(a)} for c in PS["nested_c_age_grid"] for a in PS["nested_alpha_grid"]]
nested_params = {**cv_params, "nested_c_age_grid": PS["nested_c_age_grid"],
                 "nested_alpha_grid": PS["nested_alpha_grid"],
                 "nested_inner_folds": PS["nested_inner_folds"], "nested_inner_seed": PS["nested_inner_seed"]}


def _nested():
    out = {"Z_hat": {}, "runs": [], "inner": [], "fold_settings": {}}
    for key in ("one", "two"):
        res = sc.nested_cross_validate_core_model(
            sc.features(ind, INPUTS[key]), Z, inputs=INPUTS[key], folds=FOLDS, groups=family,
            settings=SETTINGS, grid=GRID, n_inner=PS["nested_inner_folds"],
            inner_seed=PS["nested_inner_seed"], seed=PS["seed"], basis=train_bases)
        out["Z_hat"][f"mdmx_{key}"] = res["Z_hat"].astype(np.float32)
        out["Z_hat"][f"mdmxraw_{key}"] = res["Z_raw"].astype(np.float32)
        out["runs"].append(res["runs"].assign(model=f"mdmx_{key}", sex="both"))
        out["inner"].append(res["inner"].assign(model=f"mdmx_{key}"))
        out["fold_settings"][key] = res["fold_settings"]
        own = sc.cross_validate_own_sex(ind, Z, indicator_names=OWN_INPUTS[key], folds=FOLDS,
                                        groups=family, settings=SETTINGS, seed=PS["seed"],
                                        basis=train_bases, fold_settings=res["fold_settings"])
        out["Z_hat"][f"own_{key}"] = own["Z_hat"].astype(np.float32)
        out["runs"].append(own["runs"].assign(model=f"own_{key}"))
    out["runs"] = pd.concat(out["runs"], ignore_index=True)
    out["inner"] = pd.concat(out["inner"], ignore_index=True)
    return out

max_iter_used = int(all_runs["enforce_iterations"].max())
```

## Step 9 — benchmarks: Log-Quad and SVD-Comp on the same folds {#sec-c10-benchmarks}

Comparing MDMx fairly with Log-Quad and SVD-Comp needs three things: the
benchmarks must be **fitted to the same training data**, evaluated on the
**same held-out country-years**, and given the **same information**.

* **Log-Quad** (@Wilmoth2012; `mdmx.logquad`) models the log death rate
  in each five-year age group as a quadratic in $\log {}_5q_0$ plus an
  adult-mortality term $v_x k$.  It is re-estimated on each fold's training
  schedules: $a_x, b_x, c_x$ by least squares age group by age group, and
  $v_x$ as the leading singular vector of the residuals at ages 5–89.
  With ₅q₀ only, $k = 0$; with ₄₅q₁₅, $k$ is solved so that the predicted
  ₄₅q₁₅ equals the input exactly.  ₅q₀ is always reproduced exactly.
* **SVD-Comp** [@Clark2019; `mdmx.svdcomp.fit_svdcomp_classic`] takes the
  SVD of each sex's logit(q_x) matrix, keeps four components, and predicts
  their weights by regressions on eight polynomial terms in ₅q₀ and
  ₄₅q₁₅; with ₅q₀ only, ₄₅q₁₅ is first predicted from ₅q₀.  It too is
  re-estimated on each fold's training schedules, and, as published, it
  reproduces its inputs only approximately.

Using the published coefficients instead would be unfair to MDMx in the
opposite direction: both were fitted to HMD life tables that include every
population we test on.  Re-estimation is possible because the model forms
are fully specified; the re-implementations were checked during
development against the originals:
the SVD-Comp prediction code reproduces the R package `svdComp5q0` to
$10^{-14}$ when given its packaged coefficients, and Log-Quad fitted to all
observed HMD schedules recovers the published coefficients closely.
`tests/test_svdcomp.py` checks that each benchmark recovers data generated
by its own model.

**The information difference.**  Log-Quad and SVD-Comp predict each sex
from that sex's indicators only.  MDMx Models 1 and 2 use *both* sexes'
indicators — Model 1 sees the male ₅q₀ when predicting females.  That is a
legitimate feature of a joint model, but it makes a straight comparison
unfair to the benchmarks.  We therefore also report a **sex-specific MDMx
variant**: one network per sex whose only inputs are that sex's indicators,
whose loss covers only that sex's schedule, and whose predictions are
corrected to reproduce those inputs.  The table also shows MDMx at the
fixed settings of Step 6.

All methods are compared on two scales: $e_0$, and the RMSE of
$\operatorname{logit}({}_nq_x)$ over the standard abridged age groups (0,
1–4, 5–9, …, 105–109).  The abridged scale is the one on which Log-Quad is
defined, so it does not penalise Log-Quad for spreading each group's rate
evenly over single years; the single-year models are aggregated to the
same groups.

## Step 10 — the bases within each fold

The sex and age bases $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ of chapter 04 come
from every observed schedule, those of any held-out fold included.  A
cross-validation in those bases has a small leak: the bases have "seen"
the test populations, whereas Log-Quad and SVD-Comp are estimated on the
training folds alone.  Every cross-validation of this chapter therefore
re-estimates the bases from the training rows of each fit — the SVD of
those schedules, then the same smoothing and re-orthonormalisation as in
chapter 04, with the age rank held at its value on all the data.  The
check below shows what the leak would be worth: the plain
cross-validation of Models 1 and 2 at their final settings, with the
fold bases and with the bases from all the data.

```{python}
#| label: c10-basis-cv
#| code-summary: "Re-estimate the bases within each fold and cross-validate again (cached)"
def _fold_bases():
    return {k: train_bases(FOLDS != k) for k in range(PS["n_folds"])}


def _basis_cv():
    bases = _fold_bases()
    c_max = max(final_choice[k]["c_age"] for k in final_choice)
    angles, rows = [], []
    for k, (_, U_a) in bases.items():
        # Largest principal angle between the leading age components
        # estimated with and without fold k (0 = the same subspace).
        cosines = np.linalg.svd(U_age[:, :c_max].T @ U_a[:, :c_max], compute_uv=False)
        angles.append({"fold": k, "max_angle_deg": float(np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(cosines.min(), -1, 1))))})
    for key in ("one", "two"):
        settings = sc.model_settings(PS, **final_choice[key])
        for variant, basis in (("fold basis", train_bases), ("full-data basis", None)):
            res = sc.cross_validate_core_model(
                sc.features(ind, INPUTS[key]), Z, inputs=INPUTS[key], folds=FOLDS, groups=family,
                settings=settings, seed=PS["seed"], basis=basis,
                U_sex=None if basis else U_sex, U_age=None if basis else U_age)
            rows.append(sc.summarise_accuracy({f"mdmx_{key}": res["Z_hat"]}, Z, A, AGE_GROUPS, AB_STARTS)
                        .assign(variant=variant))
    return {"accuracy": pd.concat(rows, ignore_index=True), "angles": pd.DataFrame(angles)}
```

Re-estimating the bases inside each fold changes the first
`{python} PS["c_age"]` age components very little (the subspace they span
rotates by at most `{python} f"{max_angle:.1f}"` degrees); the first
`{python} c_used` move more (up to `{python} f"{angle_by_c[c_used]:.0f}"`

out-of-fold accuracy changes by at most
`{python} f"{change['e0_mae'].abs().max():.2f}"` years of $e_0$, because
the network and the correction adapt to whichever basis they are given.
The change is small, but it is of the order of the differences between
MDMx and the benchmarks in Step 9, and with the full-data bases it would
favour MDMx; that is why every cross-validation uses the fold bases.

## The final models, for the app

The final networks are trained on all `{python} f"{n_obs:,}"` observed
country-years with the settings chosen in Step 8 (Model 1:
$c_{\text{age}}$ = `{python} final_choice["one"]["c_age"]`, $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ =
`{python} f"{final_choice['one']['alpha']:g}"`; Model 2:
$c_{\text{age}}$ = `{python} final_choice["two"]["c_age"]`, $w_{\mathrm{child}}$ =
`{python} f"{final_choice['two']['alpha']:g}"`), with the same
early-stopping rule.  Each is saved as a self-contained artefact —
`svdcomp_model1` and `svdcomp_model2` — holding only arrays, numbers and
strings, so that the Shiny app (or any other program) can reproduce the
predictions exactly without this code.  Its structure is documented in the
`mdmx.svdcomp` module docstring:

| key | contents |
|---|---|
| `inputs` | input names in order; the network input is the logit of each, and these are the indicators the prediction reproduces |
| `x_scaler` | mean and scale of the logit inputs |
| `network` | layer sizes and the weight matrices and bias vectors |
| `core_scaler` | mean and scale of the core weights: core = output × scale + mean |
| `U_sex`, `U_age` | the sex basis ($2 \times 2$) and the first $c_{\text{age}}$ age components ($110 \times c_{\text{age}}$) |
| `enforce` | tolerance and iteration limit of the correction |
| `ages`, `loss_sexes`, `settings`, `history`, `description` | ages, settings and training curve |

A prediction is `mdmx.svdcomp.predict_schedules(model, X)`: the network's
core weights $\mathbf{g}_0$ = out ⊙ σ + μ for the standardised logit
inputs, the correction of Step 4, and $\hat{\mathbf{z}} = (\mathbf{S}
\otimes \mathbf{A}_c)\,\mathbf{g}^\star$; it raises an error if the
correction does not converge.  For example, for ₅q₀ = 0.05 (females) and
0.06 (males):

