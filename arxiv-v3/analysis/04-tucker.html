8.1 What the decomposition is for

The decomposition has two jobs (paper §3). The first is to find a shared basis in which every mortality schedule can be written: a sex factor matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) (\(2 \times r_1\)) and an age factor matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) (\(110 \times r_2\)) that are the same for every population and every year. Writing each schedule in that common basis gives every output of the system — model life tables, fitted schedules, predictions, forecasts — what we call shared-basis coherence: both sexes’ schedules are combinations of the same age patterns. The basis itself does not tie the two sexes together (with \(r_1 = 2\) the sex factor imposes nothing; see below); they are tied by the low-rank models built on the cores downstream, such as the forecasters’ few principal components and the regimes’ deviations. The coordinates of a country-year’s schedule in the basis form its effective core, the \(r_1 \times r_2\) matrix

\[ \mathcal{G}_{c,t} \;=\; \mathbf{S}^{\top}\, \mathcal{M}_{c,t}\, \mathbf{A}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \mathcal{M}_{c,t} \;\approx\; \mathbf{S}\, \mathcal{G}_{c,t}\, \mathbf{A}^{\top} \]

where \(\mathcal{M}_{c,t}\) is the \(2 \times 110\) matrix of \(\operatorname{logit}(q_x)\) for population \(c\) in year \(t\). Every later chapter works with these effective cores.

The second job is to describe how mortality varies across populations and over time — the country and year modes of the tensor — and, in doing so, to say something about the country-years for which we have no (non-exceptional) data. That is a completion problem: about 70% of the country-year cells are unobserved.

The two jobs are done in turn, so that values filled in for the unobserved country-years never shape the basis or the cores of the observed ones. The sex and age bases are estimated from observed schedules only, which needs no imputation, and every observed country-year is represented by its exact projection on that basis (steps 1–3). The unobserved country-years are then filled by a low-rank model of the country and year modes, fitted to observed data only by EM, with its ranks chosen by out-of-sample prediction (step 4).

8.2 Step 1 — the shared sex and age bases

Each observed country-year contributes one complete \(2 \times 110\) schedule. Stacking those schedules side by side gives the age unfolding \(\mathbf{M}_{(2)}\) (\(110 \times 2N_{\text{obs}}\)) and the sex unfolding \(\mathbf{M}_{(1)}\) (\(2 \times 110 N_{\text{obs}}\)) of the observed data, with no gaps. Their left singular vectors are the factor matrices (the paper’s shared bases, §3.2). The sex mode has only two levels and is kept at full rank, \(r_1 = 2\). The age rank \(r_2\) is the smallest number of components that captures a share \(\tau =\) 0.9999 of the variance of \(\mathbf{M}_{(2)}\) (the paper’s rank rule, in §3.2). Computing an SVD of a \(110 \times 9{,}000\) matrix is quick; we use the equivalent eigen-decomposition of the \(110 \times 110\) matrix \(\mathbf{M}_{(2)} \mathbf{M}_{(2)}^\top\) (mdmx.tensor.leading_left_vectors). Singular vectors are only defined up to sign; mdmx.tensor.fix_signs makes the largest entry of each positive so that results are identical on every computer. The first age vector is then turned, where needed, to point the same way as the mean observed schedule, so that it has the shape of a \(\operatorname{logit}(q_x)\) schedule (negative at almost every age) and its core entry rises as mortality falls.

8.3 Step 2 — smoothing the age basis

The leading age components are smooth, but the higher-order ones pick up some single-year wiggle where the data are noisy. Following paper §3.2, each column except the first is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel whose width grows with age, so that the infant peak, the childhood minimum and the young-adult “accident hump” are smoothed lightly and the old-age tail more strongly. Components 2–5 have their own settings; all higher components share one narrow, constant bandwidth. Ages 0 and 1 are reset to their unsmoothed values, and the basis is then re-orthonormalised (QR). mdmx.smoothing implements the kernel as a matrix, so smoothing a column is one matrix–vector product.

8.4 Step 3 — the effective cores of the observed country-years

With \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\) fixed, the effective core of each observed country-year is a projection, \(\mathcal{G}_{c,t} = \mathbf{S}^\top \mathcal{M}_{c,t}\mathbf{A}\), and the schedule implied by it is \(\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{c,t} = \mathbf{S}\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\mathbf{A}^\top\). The only approximation is the truncation of the age basis to \(r_2\) components, so the reconstruction of observed data is very close.

Project every observed schedule onto the shared basis; measure the fit

8.5 Step 4 — completing the unobserved country-years

To describe the country and year modes, and to say what the model implies for country-years without baseline data, we fit a Tucker model to the effective cores in which the country and year modes are compressed to ranks \(r_3\) and \(r_4\):

\[ \mathcal{G}_{c,t} \;\approx\; \mathcal{G} \times_3 \mathbf{c}_c^\top \times_4 \mathbf{t}_t^\top , \]

with \(\mathbf{C}\) (\(C \times r_3\)) and \(\mathbf{T}\) (\(T \times r_4\)) orthonormal. It is fitted to observed country-years only, by the EM algorithm (mdmx.tensor.complete_cores): start with the country means of the observed cores, fit the model, replace only the unobserved slices by the fit, and repeat until the filled values stop changing. At convergence the observed slices are data, the unobserved slices are the model’s own prediction, and the factors are those of the completed array — so the made- up values exert no pull of their own. One limit follows: a calendar year in which no population is observed — every year of the influenza pandemics and of COVID-19, for example, is exceptional in every population with data — gives its year loading nothing to be estimated from, so EM leaves it close to where it started and the model returns roughly each country’s mean schedule there (for Sweden the same e₀ in 1918 as in each of 2020–22; chapter 08). The model’s values in such years are not predictions; chapter 08 builds the baselines of exceptional years differently.

8.5.1 Choosing the ranks by prediction

A variance threshold cannot choose \(r_3\) and \(r_4\) here: most of the array is missing, and the share of variance a component “explains” depends on how the gaps were filled. Instead we ask how well each candidate pair predicts data it has not seen. A random 5% of the observed country-years is hidden, the model is fitted to the rest, and the hidden schedules are predicted.

8.5.3 The HOSVD or higher-order orthogonal iteration

Each iteration of the completion model computes \(\mathbf{C}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\) by a truncated HOSVD of the filled array: the leading left singular vectors of its country and year unfoldings. A truncated HOSVD is quasi-optimal, not optimal. Its squared error is at most the sum, over the truncated modes, of the squared singular values each one discards, so with two modes truncated its error is at most \(\sqrt{2}\) times that of the best approximation with the same ranks. Higher-order orthogonal iteration can come closer to the best: it updates each factor matrix in turn, given the other, by least squares. To see whether that would serve the completion model better, the same EM is fitted with one sweep of it in place of the HOSVD at every iteration (mdmx.hooi.complete_cores_hooi), at the selected ranks and with the same settings, and the two are compared twice: by the residual of the observed cores after the final model’s 1,500 iterations, and by how well they predict hidden country-years. The second comparison uses 10 random splits, each hiding 5% of the observed country-years as the rank search does, drawn with their own seed rather than the one whose split chose the ranks.

## What the decomposition is for

The decomposition has two jobs (paper §3).  The first is to find a **shared
basis** in which every mortality schedule can be written: a sex factor
matrix $\mathbf{S}$ ($2 \times r_1$) and an age factor matrix $\mathbf{A}$
($110 \times r_2$) that are the same for every population and every year.
Writing each schedule in that common basis gives every output of the
system — model life tables, fitted schedules, predictions, forecasts — what
we call *shared-basis coherence*: both sexes' schedules are combinations of
the same age patterns.  The basis itself does not tie the two sexes together
(with $r_1 = 2$ the sex factor imposes nothing; see below); they are tied by
the low-rank models built on the cores downstream, such as the forecasters'
few principal components and the regimes' deviations.  The coordinates of a country-year's
schedule in the basis form its **effective core**, the $r_1 \times r_2$
matrix

$$
\mathcal{G}_{c,t} \;=\; \mathbf{S}^{\top}\, \mathcal{M}_{c,t}\, \mathbf{A},
\qquad\text{so that}\qquad
\mathcal{M}_{c,t} \;\approx\; \mathbf{S}\, \mathcal{G}_{c,t}\, \mathbf{A}^{\top}
$$

where $\mathcal{M}_{c,t}$ is the $2 \times 110$ matrix of
$\operatorname{logit}(q_x)$ for population $c$ in year $t$.  Every later
chapter works with these effective cores.

The second job is to describe how mortality varies **across populations
and over time** — the country and year modes of the tensor — and, in doing
so, to say something about the country-years for which we have no
(non-exceptional) data.  That is a *completion* problem: about 70% of the
country-year cells are unobserved.

The two jobs are done in turn, so that values filled in for the unobserved
country-years never shape the basis or the cores of the observed ones.  The
sex and age bases are estimated from observed schedules only, which needs
no imputation, and every observed country-year is represented by its exact
projection on that basis (steps 1–3).  The unobserved country-years are
then filled by a low-rank model of the country and year modes, fitted to
observed data only by EM, with its ranks chosen by out-of-sample prediction
(step 4).

## Step 1 — the shared sex and age bases

Each observed country-year contributes one complete $2 \times 110$ schedule.
Stacking those schedules side by side gives the age unfolding
$\mathbf{M}_{(2)}$ ($110 \times 2N_{\text{obs}}$) and the sex unfolding
$\mathbf{M}_{(1)}$ ($2 \times 110 N_{\text{obs}}$) of the *observed* data,
with no gaps.  Their left singular vectors are the factor matrices
(the paper's shared bases, §3.2).  The sex mode has only two levels and is kept at full rank,
$r_1 = 2$.  The age rank $r_2$ is the smallest number of components that
captures a share $\tau =$ `{python} P.tucker.tau` of the variance of
$\mathbf{M}_{(2)}$ (the paper's rank rule, in §3.2).  Computing an SVD of a $110 \times 9{,}000$
matrix is quick; we use the equivalent eigen-decomposition of the
$110 \times 110$ matrix $\mathbf{M}_{(2)} \mathbf{M}_{(2)}^\top$
(`mdmx.tensor.leading_left_vectors`).  Singular vectors are only defined up
to sign; `mdmx.tensor.fix_signs` makes the largest entry of each positive so
that results are identical on every computer.  The first age vector is then
turned, where needed, to point the same way as the mean observed schedule, so
that it has the shape of a $\operatorname{logit}(q_x)$ schedule (negative at almost every
age) and its core entry rises as mortality falls.

## Step 2 — smoothing the age basis

The leading age components are smooth, but the higher-order ones pick up
some single-year wiggle where the data are noisy.  Following paper §3.2,
each column except the first is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel whose width
grows with age, so that the infant peak, the childhood minimum and
the young-adult "accident hump" are smoothed lightly and the old-age tail
more strongly.  Components 2–5 have their own settings; all higher
components share one narrow, constant bandwidth.  Ages 0 and 1 are reset to
their unsmoothed values, and the basis is then re-orthonormalised (QR).
`mdmx.smoothing` implements the kernel as a matrix, so smoothing a column
is one matrix–vector product.

## Step 3 — the effective cores of the observed country-years

With $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ fixed, the effective core of each
observed country-year is a projection, $\mathcal{G}_{c,t} =
\mathbf{S}^\top \mathcal{M}_{c,t}\mathbf{A}$, and the schedule implied by
it is $\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{c,t} = \mathbf{S}\mathcal{G}_{c,t}\mathbf{A}^\top$.
The only approximation is the truncation of the age basis to $r_2$
components, so the reconstruction of observed data is very close.

## Step 4 — completing the unobserved country-years

To describe the country and year modes, and to say what the model implies
for country-years without baseline data, we fit a Tucker model to the
effective cores in which the country and year modes are compressed to ranks
$r_3$ and $r_4$:

$$
\mathcal{G}_{c,t} \;\approx\; \mathcal{G} \times_3 \mathbf{c}_c^\top \times_4 \mathbf{t}_t^\top ,
$$

with $\mathbf{C}$ ($C \times r_3$) and $\mathbf{T}$ ($T \times r_4$)
orthonormal.  It is fitted to observed country-years only, by the EM
algorithm (`mdmx.tensor.complete_cores`): start with the country means
of the observed cores, fit the model, replace *only* the unobserved slices by the
fit, and repeat until the filled values stop changing.  At convergence the
observed slices are data, the unobserved slices are the model's own
prediction, and the factors are those of the completed array — so the made-
up values exert no pull of their own.  One limit follows: a calendar year in
which *no* population is observed — every year of the influenza pandemics and
of COVID-19, for example, is exceptional in every population with data — gives
its year loading nothing to be estimated from, so EM leaves it close to where
it started and the model returns roughly each country's mean schedule there
(for Sweden the same e₀ in 1918 as in each of 2020–22; chapter 08).  The model's values
in such years are not predictions; chapter 08 builds the baselines of
exceptional years differently.

### Choosing the ranks by prediction

A variance threshold cannot choose $r_3$ and $r_4$ here: most of the array
is missing, and the share of variance a component "explains" depends on how
the gaps were filled.  Instead we ask how well each candidate pair predicts
data it has not seen.  A random `{python} f"{100 * P.tucker.holdout_share:.0f}"`% of the
observed country-years is hidden, the model is fitted to the rest, and the
hidden schedules are predicted.

### The HOSVD or higher-order orthogonal iteration

Each iteration of the completion model computes $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{T}$ by a truncated
HOSVD of the filled array: the leading left singular vectors of its country and year unfoldings.
A truncated HOSVD is quasi-optimal, not optimal.  Its squared error is at most the sum, over the
truncated modes, of the squared singular values each one discards, so with two modes truncated its
error is at most $\sqrt{2}$ times that of the best approximation with the same ranks.
Higher-order orthogonal iteration can come closer to the best: it updates each factor matrix in
turn, given the other, by least squares.  To see whether that would serve the completion model
better, the same EM is fitted with one sweep of it in place of the HOSVD at every iteration
(`mdmx.hooi.complete_cores_hooi`), at the selected ranks and with the same settings, and the two are
compared twice: by the residual of the observed cores after the final model's
`{python} f"{P.tucker.em_max_iter:,}"` iterations, and by how well they predict hidden country-years.
The second comparison uses `{python} P.section("checks")["hooi_splits"]` random splits, each hiding
`{python} f"{100 * P.tucker.holdout_share:.0f}"`% of the observed country-years as the rank search
does, drawn with their own seed rather than the one whose split chose the ranks.

```{python}
#| label: c04-hooi
#| code-summary: "The completion model with higher-order orthogonal iteration (cached; about a minute)"
CK = P.section("checks")


def _hooi_check():
    done, fac, core, hist = hooi.complete_cores_hooi(Y_obs, observed, r3, r4,
                                                     max_iter=P.tucker.em_max_iter, tol=P.tucker.em_tol)
    fit = pd.DataFrame({
        "method": ["HOSVD", "HOOI"],
        "resid_obs": [hooi.observed_residual(comp["G_ct"], observed, [comp["U_country"], comp["U_year"]],
                                             comp["core"]),
                      hooi.observed_residual(done, observed, fac, core)],
        "iterations": [len(comp["history"]), len(hist)]})
    rng = np.random.default_rng(CK["hooi_seed"])
    ci, ti = np.nonzero(observed)
    rows = []
    for split in range(CK["hooi_splits"]):
        pick = rng.choice(len(ci), size=max(1, int(round(P.tucker.holdout_share * len(ci)))), replace=False)
        hidden = np.zeros_like(observed)
        hidden[ci[pick], ti[pick]] = True
        for name, complete in (("HOSVD", tz.complete_cores), ("HOOI", hooi.complete_cores_hooi)):
            Yc = complete(Y_obs, observed & ~hidden, r3, r4, max_iter=P.tucker.search_max_iter,
                          tol=P.tucker.em_tol)[0]
            rows.append({"split": split + 1, "method": name, **_loss(Yc, hidden)})
    return {"fit": fit, "holdout": pd.DataFrame(rows)}


hc = artifacts.stage("tucker_hooi_check", _hooi_check,
                     inputs=["tensor", "tucker_bases", "tucker_age_smoothed", "tucker_completion"],
                     params={"r3": r3, "r4": r4, "em_max_iter": P.tucker.em_max_iter,
                             "search_max_iter": P.tucker.search_max_iter, "em_tol": P.tucker.em_tol,
                             "holdout_share": P.tucker.holdout_share, "splits": CK["hooi_splits"],
                             "seed": CK["hooi_seed"]},
                     code=[_hooi_check, _loss, hooi.complete_cores_hooi, hooi.observed_residual,
                           tz.complete_cores],
                     desc="The completion model with higher-order orthogonal iteration in place of the HOSVD: "
                          "fit to the observed cores and prediction of hidden country-years")
res = hc["fit"].set_index("method")["resid_obs"]
hooi_gain = 100 * (1 - res["HOOI"] / res["HOSVD"])
ho = hc["holdout"].pivot(index="split", columns="method", values=["rmse", "e0_mae"])
hooi_worse = int((ho[("rmse", "HOOI")] > ho[("rmse", "HOSVD")]).sum())
hooi_worse_e0 = int((ho[("e0_mae", "HOOI")] > ho[("e0_mae", "HOSVD")]).sum())
mean = ho.mean()
print(f"Residual of the observed cores: HOSVD {res['HOSVD']:.2f}, HOOI {res['HOOI']:.2f} ({hooi_gain:.1f} % lower)")
print(f"Hold-out, mean of {CK['hooi_splits']} splits: RMSE HOSVD {mean[('rmse', 'HOSVD')]:.3f}, "
      f"HOOI {mean[('rmse', 'HOOI')]:.3f} (HOOI worse in {hooi_worse}); e0 MAE HOSVD "
      f"{mean[('e0_mae', 'HOSVD')]:.2f}, HOOI {mean[('e0_mae', 'HOOI')]:.2f} y (HOOI worse in {hooi_worse_e0})")
latex.report("tuckerHOOIResidGain", hooi_gain, digits=1,
             desc="Residual of the observed cores, % lower with higher-order orthogonal iteration than with the HOSVD")
latex.report("tuckerHOSVDHoldRMSE", float(mean[("rmse", "HOSVD")]), digits=3,
             desc="Hold-out RMSE (logit), completion model with the HOSVD, mean of the random splits")
latex.report("tuckerHOOIHoldRMSE", float(mean[("rmse", "HOOI")]), digits=3,
             desc="Hold-out RMSE (logit), completion model with higher-order orthogonal iteration, mean of the splits")
latex.report("tuckerHOSVDHoldMAE", float(mean[("e0_mae", "HOSVD")]), digits=2,
             desc="Hold-out e0 MAE (years), completion model with the HOSVD, mean of the random splits")
latex.report("tuckerHOOIHoldMAE", float(mean[("e0_mae", "HOOI")]), digits=2,
             desc="Hold-out e0 MAE (years), completion model with higher-order orthogonal iteration, mean of the splits")
latex.report("tuckerHOOIWorseSplits", hooi_worse, desc="Random splits in which HOOI's hold-out RMSE is higher")
latex.report("tuckerHOOIWorseSplitsMAE", hooi_worse_e0, desc="Random splits in which HOOI's hold-out e0 MAE is higher")
latex.report("tuckerHOOISplits", int(CK["hooi_splits"]), desc="Random hold-out splits of the HOSVD/HOOI comparison")
ho.round(3)
```

Higher-order orthogonal iteration fits the observed cores more closely, as it must: one sweep never
moves the model away from the filled array, so the residual of the observed cores cannot rise from
one iteration to the next.  The closer fit does not carry over to country-years the model has not
seen: in `{python} hooi_worse` of the `{python} CK["hooi_splits"]` splits it predicts them less
well than the HOSVD.  The completion model keeps the HOSVD, which also keeps each iteration in
closed form.
