11.1 What this chapter does

The decomposition and the regimes describe the slow, secular change of mortality. Wars and epidemics are short, sharp departures from it, so the country-years they affect were set aside (chapter d4) and are modelled here. Paper §6 writes an exceptional country-year as

\[ y_{s,a,c,t} \;=\; \hat y_{s,a,c,t} + \lambda_{c,t}\,\delta_{d,s,a}, \]

its baseline \(\hat y\) — the schedule it would have had without the event — plus a disruption: the profile \(\boldsymbol\delta_d\) of the event’s type \(d\) (a unit vector over sex and age) scaled by an intensity \(\lambda_{c,t}\). Everything rests on the baseline, which is never observed. The chapter chooses it by asking which candidate best recovers schedules we do know, then estimates the profiles and intensities, checks how well one profile per type describes the events, and shows what a disruption does to life expectancy.

11.3 Step 2 — the baseline, chosen on pseudo-events

The baseline of an exceptional year is the schedule the population would have had without the event. The candidates (paper §6.1; the first two never read the exceptional year’s own schedule):

  • temporal interpolation of the population’s effective cores (paper §6.1) — linear between the nearest observed years on either side (at a series end either carrying the last core or extending a 10-year trend), or a local-linear smoother in calendar year over ±5, ±10, ±20 years;
  • the neural core (a reference candidate, not in the paper): a network from the country’s loading and nine features of calendar year to the core, trained on the observed country-years, with early stopping on whole held-out calendar years;
  • the naïve projection (paper §6.1): the schedule in the population’s slice of the completion model closest to the observed exceptional schedule;
  • the penalised projection (paper §6.1, Eq. 15): the same, pulled towards an interpolation with weight α (one of 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100). As α grows it tends to the projection of the interpolation onto that slice, not to the interpolation itself;
  • the completion model of chapter 04, for reference.

To compare them on schedules whose truth is known, every event is moved, as one block across all its populations, to the normal years just beyond its own span on each side (mdmx.disruption.pseudo_events). Those pseudo-events are hidden from every candidate; a spell with data on one side only keeps that property, and an event that hits every population leaves no population observed in the shifted year either. A normal year that chapter d5 pooled with its neighbours is not used: they carry its deaths, which the neighbours of a real exceptional year never do. The completion model and the neural core are refitted without the hidden years. Each pseudo-event then receives the residual of a real exceptional country-year of the same type from another population family (mdmx.disruption.inject; the residuals come from linear interpolation), and every candidate estimates the baseline from the disrupted schedule. The score is the RMSE of logit(\(q_x\)) between the estimate and the clean schedule, over both sexes and all ages. The rule, fixed before the run: the candidate with the lowest pooled RMSE over the injected pseudo-events is used. The same candidates are also scored without injection, for comparison.

11.4 Step 3 — baselines and residuals of the exceptional years

The chosen candidate is applied to every exceptional country-year, using all observed years (and the completion model of chapter 04, fitted without the exceptional years). The residual is the observed minus the baseline logit(\(q_x\)) (paper §6.1). A cell is left out when its observed \(q_x\) was zero (floored) or when the deaths expected under the linear interpolation — HMD exposure × its death rate — are below 5; the second rule removes the upward bias of keeping only the small cells that happened to have deaths, without looking at the deaths themselves.

11.5 Step 4 — profiles and intensities

The profile of a type is its mean residual scaled to unit length (paper §6.2), smoothed along age by a Savitzky–Golay filter (window 11 ages, degree 3) for each sex with the end ages kept, and rescaled. The type “other” (6 country-years of famine, measles and smallpox) is too heterogeneous for a profile; its years keep their residuals. The intensity of a country-year is the least-squares coefficient of its residual on its type’s profile (paper §6.2), over the cells not left out.

11.6 Step 5 — one profile per type, per event, or per sub-cluster?

Step 4 estimates a single profile per type. The heatmaps suggest that events of one type differ, and Ward sub-clusters of the residuals are one way to capture it. Three alternatives are compared, each estimated without the population family it is scored on (mdmx.disruption.holdout_heterogeneity): (a) one profile per type; (b) one profile per historical event — the dictionary entries grouped into wars and pandemics (World War I, World War II, 1918, COVID-19, the cholera pandemics, …) — where the event has at least 10 country-years in the training families, the type’s profile otherwise; (c) Ward sub-clusters (PCA to at most 20 components, the first 10 clustered, K from 2–7 by silhouette, sub-clusters of at least 5), with a held-out country-year fitted by whichever sub-profile fits it best — a choice made with the held-out residual itself, which favours (c). The score is the pooled explained share of the held-out residuals’ sum of squares.

## What this chapter does

The decomposition and the regimes describe the slow, secular change of
mortality.  Wars and epidemics are short, sharp departures from it, so the
country-years they affect were set aside (chapter d4) and are modelled
here.  Paper §6 writes an exceptional country-year as

$$
y_{s,a,c,t} \;=\; \hat y_{s,a,c,t} + \lambda_{c,t}\,\delta_{d,s,a},
$$

its **baseline** $\hat y$ — the schedule it would have had without the
event — plus a **disruption**: the profile $\boldsymbol\delta_d$ of the
event's type $d$ (a unit vector over sex and age) scaled by an
**intensity** $\lambda_{c,t}$.  Everything rests on the baseline, which is
never observed.  The chapter chooses it by asking which candidate best
recovers schedules we *do* know, then estimates the profiles and
intensities, checks how well one profile per type describes the events,

## Step 2 — the baseline, chosen on pseudo-events

The baseline of an exceptional year is the schedule the population would
have had without the event.  The candidates (paper §6.1; the first two
never read the exceptional year's own schedule):

* **temporal interpolation** of the population's effective cores (paper
  §6.1) — *linear* between
  the nearest observed years on either side (at a series end either
  carrying the last core or extending a
  `{python} f"{PE['trend_years']}"`-year trend), or a *local-linear*
  smoother in calendar year over
  ±`{python} ", ±".join(f"{w:g}" for w in PE["local_windows"])` years;
* the **neural core** (a reference candidate, not in the paper): a network from
  the country's loading and nine features of calendar year to the core,
  trained on the observed country-years, with early stopping on whole
  held-out calendar years;
* the **naïve projection** (paper §6.1): the schedule in the
  population's slice of the completion model closest to the observed
  exceptional schedule;
* the **penalised projection** (paper §6.1, Eq. 15): the same, pulled
  towards an interpolation with weight α (one of
  `{python} ", ".join(f"{a:g}" for a in PE["alphas"])`).  As α grows it tends
  to the *projection* of the interpolation onto that slice, not to the
  interpolation itself;
* the **completion model** of chapter 04, for reference.

To compare them on schedules whose truth is known, every event is moved, as
one block across all its populations, to the normal years just beyond its
own span on each side (`mdmx.disruption.pseudo_events`).  Those
**pseudo-events** are hidden from every candidate; a spell with data on one
side only keeps that property, and an event that hits every population
leaves no population observed in the shifted year either.  A normal year
that chapter d5 pooled with its neighbours is not used: they carry its
deaths, which the neighbours of a real exceptional year never do.  The completion
model and the neural core are refitted without the hidden years.  Each
pseudo-event then receives the residual of a real exceptional country-year
of the same type from another population family (`mdmx.disruption.inject`;
the residuals come from linear interpolation), and every candidate
estimates the baseline from the disrupted schedule.  The score is the
**RMSE of logit($q_x$)** between the estimate and the clean schedule, over
both sexes and all ages.  The rule, fixed before the run: **the candidate
with the lowest pooled RMSE over the injected pseudo-events is used**.  The
same candidates are also scored without injection, for comparison.

The chosen candidate is applied to every exceptional country-year, using
all observed years (and the completion model of chapter 04, fitted without
the exceptional years).  The residual is the observed minus the baseline
logit($q_x$) (paper §6.1).  A cell is left out when its observed
$q_x$ was zero (floored) or when the deaths *expected* under the linear
interpolation — HMD exposure × its death rate — are below
`{python} f"{PE['min_expected_deaths']:g}"`; the second rule removes the
upward bias of keeping only the small cells that happened to have deaths,
without looking at the deaths themselves.

## Step 4 — profiles and intensities

The profile of a type is its mean residual scaled to unit length (paper
§6.2), smoothed along age by a
Savitzky–Golay filter (window `{python} f"{PP['sg_window']}"` ages, degree
`{python} f"{PP['sg_poly']}"`) for each sex with the end ages kept, and
rescaled.  The type "other"
(`{python} f"{int(n_type['other'])}"` country-years of famine, measles and
smallpox) is too heterogeneous for a profile; its years keep their
residuals.  The intensity of a country-year is the least-squares
coefficient of its residual on its type's profile (paper §6.2), over the
cells not left out.

being within ±`{python} num("excChildOtherRespFemale")`.  The **enteric** profile is
raised over childhood and adult ages, peaking at
`{python} num("excPeakEntericFemale")` (women) and
`{python} num("excPeakEntericMale")` (men), not concentrated in infants and
young children as cholera would suggest.
Smoothing changes the profiles little — cosines of raw and smoothed
`{python} num("excCosWar")` (war), `{python} num("excCosResp")`
(respiratory) and `{python} num("excCosEnteric")` (enteric) — but the
cosine exceeds `{python} f"{PFIG['paper_cosine']:g}"` only for
`{python} " and ".join(_cos_ok) or "none"`, not for `{python} " and ".join(_cos_bad) or "any type"`.

The null bands are wide — an intensity must exceed
`{python} num("excNullHighWar")` for a war,
`{python} num("excNullHighResp")` for a respiratory and
`{python} num("excNullHighEnteric")` for an enteric pandemic to stand out
from the year-to-year noise of a baseline.  Of the
`{python} num("excNNeg")` negative intensities only
`{python} num("excNBelow")` fall below their band: almost all are
country-years whose event left no detectable mark, not deficits.  That is
common: `{python} num("excNAboveWar")` of
`{python} num("excNWar")` war country-years and
`{python} num("excNAboveResp")` of `{python} num("excNResp")` respiratory
ones lie above the band.  By event (table above), the influenza pandemics
of 1889–90, 1957–58 and 1968–69 barely register at annual resolution;
1918 and the World Wars do.  The Boer and Korean Wars — expeditionary wars
fought far from home — have a median intensity of
`{python} num("excLamExpeditionary")` and no country-year above the band;
leaving them out changes the war profile very little (cosine
`{python} num("excCosNoExpeditionary")`).

## Step 5 — one profile per type, per event, or per sub-cluster?

Step 4 estimates a single profile per type.  The heatmaps suggest that
events of one type differ, and Ward sub-clusters of the residuals are one
way to capture it.  Three alternatives are compared, each
estimated without the population family it is scored on
(`mdmx.disruption.holdout_heterogeneity`): **(a)** one profile per type;
**(b)** one profile per historical event — the dictionary entries grouped
into wars and pandemics (World War I, World War II, 1918, COVID-19, the
cholera pandemics, …) — where the event has at least
`{python} f"{PH['min_event_n']}"` country-years in the training families,
the type's profile otherwise; **(c)** Ward sub-clusters
(PCA to at most `{python} f"{PH['max_pca']}"` components, the first
`{python} f"{PH['n_use']}"` clustered, K from
`{python} f"{min(PH['k_range'])}"`–`{python} f"{max(PH['k_range'])}"` by
silhouette, sub-clusters of at least `{python} f"{PH['min_size']}"`), with a
held-out country-year fitted by whichever sub-profile fits it best — a
choice made with the held-out residual itself, which favours (c).  The
score is the pooled explained share of the held-out residuals' sum of
squares.
