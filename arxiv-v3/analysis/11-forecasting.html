14.1 What is forecast

Both forecasters forecast a population’s complete mortality schedule: single-year logit(q_x) for ages 0–109, female and male, every year ahead. Both work in the same space, the first principal components of the effective cores (Step 1 below), and both start from the population’s last observed schedule. They differ in how they move through that space: MDMx extrapolates each population’s own recent trend, damped and pulled towards the HMD-wide drift; the flow field moves every population along the paths that populations at the same level of mortality followed in the past.

TipWhat “e₀” means in this chapter and the next

Wherever an unqualified e₀ is quoted (an error, a bias, a coverage), it is the average of the female and the male e₀, \(\tfrac12(e_0^F + e_0^M)\), not the e₀ of a combined-sex life table.

14.2 The MDMx forecaster, step by step

14.2.1 Step 1 — the space, re-estimated at every origin

Every observed schedule is represented by its effective core \(\G_{c,t} = \bS^\top \M_{c,t}\, \bA\) (chapter 04), an \(r_1 \times r_2\) matrix with 2 × 50 = 100 entries. Forecasting 100 numbers jointly is wasteful, because they move together: a principal-component analysis (PCA) of the vectorised cores of all observed country-years (paper §9.1, Eq. 24),

\[ \mathbf{s}_{c,t} = \mathbf{V}^\top\big(\operatorname{vec}(\G_{c,t}) - \bar{\mathbf{g}}\big) \in \R^{N_{PC}}, \]

with \(N_{PC}\) = 5 components, reduces each core to a handful of scores, and the map back to a schedule is linear (paper Eq. 25): \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{mean}} + \mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{s}\), where column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{L}\) is the female and male \(\logit(q_x)\) schedule \(\bS\,\mathbf{V}_j\,\bA^\top\) of the \(j\)-th loading.

A forecast made at origin \(o\) must not use any data after \(o\). The bases \(\bS\) and \(\bA\), the smoothing of \(\bA\), the effective cores and the PCA are therefore all re-estimated at each origin from the observed country-years up to that origin (mdmx.forecast.origin_space), exactly as chapter 04 estimates them from all the data. The age rank is held at the full-data value, \(r_2\) = 50, at every origin, so that the forecasts from different origins have the same structure. For the latest year in the tensor this reproduces the decomposition of chapter 04 exactly, which the next cell checks.

14.2.2 Step 2 — the drift target

Each population’s own recent trend is informative, but noisy; the average trend of all HMD populations is stable, but ignores what is particular to the population. MDMx blends the two (paper §9.2.1):

\[ \boldsymbol\delta_{\text{hier}} = w_{\text{HMD}}\,\boldsymbol\delta_{\text{HMD}} + w_{\text{country}}\,\boldsymbol\delta_{c}, \]

with weights \(w_{\text{HMD}}\) = 0.8 and \(w_{\text{country}}\) = 0.2. A population’s drift \(\boldsymbol\delta_c\) is the average change of its scores per calendar year over the window \([o-W, o]\), with \(W\) = 20 years, measured between its first and last observed years in the window (mdmx.forecast.country_drifts); missing and exceptional years inside the window are simply not used, and the elapsed time is counted in calendar years. A population needs at least 10 observed years in the window for its drift to count. \(\boldsymbol\delta_{\text{HMD}}\) is the average of the drifts of all populations with a defined drift at that origin. The target has no regime level (paper §9.2.1), so this chapter does not depend on the clustering of chapter 06.

14.2.3 Step 3 — the drift-constrained Kalman filter

For each PCA score the model is a damped local linear trend whose drift reverts not to zero but to the target (paper §9.2.2, Eqs. 26–27):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + \delta_{t-1} + \eta^{\ell}_t, &\operatorname{var}\eta^\ell &= q^\ell,\\ \delta_t &= \rho\,\delta_{t-1} + (1-\rho)\,\delta_{\text{hier}} + \eta^{\delta}_t, &\operatorname{var}\eta^\delta &= q^\delta,\\ y_t &= \ell_t + \varepsilon_t, &\operatorname{var}\varepsilon &= r, \end{aligned} \]

where \(y_t\) is the observed score in calendar year \(t\). With \(\rho<1\) the forecast drift converges geometrically to the target, so a long-range forecast follows the blended trend whatever the population did in its last few years. The covariances are diagonal, so the 5 components are independent two-state filters that share \(\rho\); mdmx.forecast.llt_filter writes this out and is checked against the textbook matrix filter in tests/test_forecast.py.

The filter runs over every calendar year from the population’s first observed year to the origin. In a year without an observed schedule — a war or epidemic year set aside as exceptional, or a year missing from the HMD series — the update step is skipped: the state is carried forward by the model and its uncertainty grows, exactly as in a forecast. The \(3N_{PC}+1\) = 16 parameters (\(q^\ell\), \(q^\delta\), \(r\) for each component, and the common \(\rho\), kept between 0.8 and 0.999) maximise the Gaussian likelihood of the observed years (prediction-error decomposition) by L-BFGS-B. Many of these maxima lie on a bound — a variance at its floor, which is effectively zero, or \(\rho\) at its lower bound — so the optimiser works on the standard deviations and on \(\rho\) itself, where such a maximum can be reached. It runs until \(-\log L\) stops decreasing at machine precision, and a fit counts as converged if the projected gradient there is below 0.001. The likelihood can have more than one local maximum — typically one with \(\rho\) at its lower bound and a higher one inside the range — so the optimiser is started from several points and the best fit is kept: moment-based starting values (\(q^\ell = \operatorname{var}\Delta y\), \(q^\delta = \operatorname{var}\Delta^2 y/4\), \(r = \operatorname{var}\Delta y/10\), within their bounds) with \(\rho\) in the middle of its range and at 7 fixed levels across it, and the 3 best points of the profile likelihood of \(\rho\) on a grid of 12 values. Given \(\rho\), the components are independent, so the profile is cheap: the standard deviations are found component by component by a grid search and then fitted exactly at every grid value of \(\rho\) at once. Several starts make a missed maximum unlikely, not impossible; the design was chosen by comparing search strategies. Chapter 12 reports convergence and the fitted \(\rho\) by origin for every fit of the evaluation, under both fitting periods. How often a single start would have missed the maximum is counted below for the fits of the long-run forecasts, one per population at the latest data year.

14.2.4 Step 4 — from scores to schedules and e₀

A forecast of the scores is a forecast of the complete female and male schedules, \(\hat{\mathbf z}_{o+h} = \mathbf z_{\text{mean}} + \mathbf L\, \hat{\mathbf s}_{o+h}\), and from those of e₀ for each sex. The forecast uncertainty of the scores at horizon \(h\) is that of a future observed score: the filter’s state variance plus the observation noise \(r\) (both diagonal). Because e₀ is a nonlinear function of the schedule, its standard deviation follows from the delta method (paper §9.2): \(\sigma^2_{e_0}(h) = \sum_j J_j^2 \operatorname{var}(s_j)\) with \(J = \partial e_0/\partial \mathbf s = (\partial e_0/\partial\mathbf z)\,\mathbf L\). The gradient \(\partial e_0/\partial\mathbf z\) is computed exactly from the life table (mdmx.forecast.e0_and_gradient): with \(e_0 = \sum_k w_k l_k\), \(\partial e_0/\partial z_x = -q_x\sum_{k>x} w_k l_k\). A Monte-Carlo check of the delta method is reported with the intervals.

14.2.5 Step 5 — the jump-off anchor

The Kalman filter starts the forecast from the filtered level of the scores, and the 5 scores represent a schedule only up to the PCA truncation. The schedule the forecast starts from therefore differs from the observed one, by a small amount at most ages but enough to move the female − male gap of e₀ at the jump-off. MDMx keeps the part of the observed schedule that its scores do not represent (paper §9.2.4, Eq. 28): the residual of the observed schedule at the job’s last training year from its projection on the scores, \(\varepsilon_{c} = \mathrm{logit}\,q_{c,t_0} - (\bar z + L\,s_{c,t_0})\), is added to every forecast schedule (forecast.mdmx_origin(anchor="persistent"), the persistent anchor, ρ = 1). The flow field uses the same anchor. A decaying anchor, with a half-life of 2 years, is a sensitivity in chapter 12.

The residual holds two things: the population’s own detail, which the anchor is there to keep, and the noise of that one year’s single-age rates, which it is not. Carried as observed, the noise would stay in every forecast schedule at every horizon. The residual is therefore smoothed over age before it is added, each sex’s by a Whittaker smoother with second differences, \(\tilde\varepsilon = \arg\min_{u} \sum_x (u_x - \varepsilon_x)^2 + \nu \sum_x (\Delta^2 u_x)^2\) over ages 1 and over, with ν = 1,000; age 0 keeps its observed value, because the step from infancy to childhood is real (forecast.smooth_residual). Both forecasters carry \(\tilde\varepsilon_c\). The smoothing was chosen on the evaluation’s origins up to 2000, among smoothers over age and over years, of the sex difference and of each sex; chapter 12 shows what it does to the comparison’s measures.

At every origin of the evaluation, the cell measures what the anchor keeps: the e₀ and the gap of the projection plus the smoothed residual minus those of the projection, and the share of the residual that the smoothing removes.

14.3 The flow field

The flow field (paper §9.3) forecasts in MDMx’s space (Step 1), from data up to the origin only (mdmx.flowfield). Its state is the vector of PCA scores of a population-year, \(s = (s_1, \dots, s_5)\); the first score is the level of mortality, oriented so that a higher \(s_1\) is lower mortality.

  1. The speed (flowfield.speed_function). Each population’s \(s_1\) is smoothed over its observed years, and its annual change is computed. The speed \(g(s_1)\) is a LOWESS of these changes against \(s_1\), pooled over the populations: how fast populations at each level of mortality improved. It is never allowed to reverse the improvement.
  2. The frontier point (flowfield.orientation). \(s_1^*\) is the median \(s_1\) of the population-years whose e₀ is in the top 10%. Below it a forecast moves at the pace that populations at its level moved in the past. Beyond it there is no past to learn from.
  3. The pace beyond the frontier (flowfield.frontier_level, frontier_speed, frontier_rule). In each year the 3 populations with the highest e₀ lead; their mean \(s_1\), chain-linked so that populations entering the data do not move it, gives the frontier’s pace \(V_s\) over the 50 years before the origin. Beyond \(s_1^*\) the speed is \(v_1 = \max\{0,\, w V_s + (1 - w)\, g(s_1^*)\}\), with \(w = n/(n + n_0)\), \(n_0\) = 3, for the \(n\) populations that led in those years.
  4. The shape law (flowfield.shape_law). The other scores follow \(s_1\) along \(f_k(s_1)\), a LOWESS of \(s_k\) on \(s_1\) over the last 50 years (along its tangent beyond the frontier point of those years), and a population’s own deviation from \(f_k\) decays at the pooled rate \(\alpha_k\) a year.
  5. The path (flowfield.integrate, forecast_origin). From the last observed scores, \(s_1\) moves one year at a time at the speed of its level, the other scores follow the shape law, and the schedules carry the jump-off anchor with its smoothed residual (Step 5).

The form (the speed on every year, the shape law on the last 50, the leaders’ pace beyond the frontier) was chosen in development studies on the development origins; chapter 12 describes how (the article’s §10.9 says on which origins the form was selected). 14.3.1 What the fitted flow says about mortality change

The flow field’s fitted objects can be read as statements about how mortality changes. Read together, the speed and the shape law trace one path through the space of schedules, the typical path: the level \(s_1\) moves at the speed \(g(s_1)\) and the other scores sit on the shape law. One year along it the level moves to \(s_1 + g(s_1)\), so the gain in e₀ that the flow implies at the level \(s_1\) is \(e(s_1 + g(s_1)) - e(s_1)\), where \(e(s_1)\) is the e₀ of the typical schedule at that level (paper §11.4, Eq. 37). Plotted against e₀, this implied gain is the flow field’s phase portrait of life expectancy: the counterpart of bayesLife’s gain function, estimated over the whole schedule and without a parametric form. The typical path also gives each sex’s e₀, so the gap between the sexes along the level of mortality.

The section describes the whole transition, so it uses the flow at the latest data year with the shape law estimated on every year (config/params.d/theory.toml). The forecaster’s shape law uses the last 50 years only, when few populations were at the lower levels. Observed gains are centred over 10 years.

# The dip of the implied gain inside a band of e0, and its recovery up to the frontier point.
def dip(path, band, front):
    """The smallest implied gain with e0 inside ``band`` and its e0; then the largest gain after it up to the
    frontier point, and its e0 (the recovery)."""
    seg = path[path["e0"].between(*band)]
    i = seg["gain"].idxmin()
    low, low_e0 = float(seg.loc[i, "gain"]), float(seg.loc[i, "e0"])
    after = path[(path["e0"] > low_e0) & (path["e0"] <= front)]
    j = after["gain"].idxmax()
    return low, low_e0, float(after.loc[j, "gain"]), float(after.loc[j, "e0"])

14.6 Sex differences in the long run

MDMx forecasts both sexes at every age from one set of scores, so the age pattern of the sex difference is forecast jointly — not held fixed by assumption, as in the coherent FDM (whose sex ratios are stationary), nor left free, as in Lee–Carter and Hyndman–Ullah. How does it behave far ahead? The forecast drift of the scores reverts to the hierarchical target δ_hier (Step 3), so in the long run the male − female difference of logit(q_x) at age x changes at the constant rate \((L_{\text{male}} - L_{\text{female}})\,\delta_{\text{hier}}\) (mdmx.compare.sex_difference_slope). On the logit scale any non-zero rate means that the difference keeps changing, so the question is how fast. The cell computes the rate for every population at the latest data year, 2025 — split into the part that comes from the HMD-wide drift (common to the populations) and the population’s own part — and compares the change it implies over 100 years with the change actually observed over each population’s last 50 years (as pre-specified).

## What is forecast

Both forecasters forecast a population's *complete* mortality schedule:
single-year logit(q_x) for ages 0–109, female and male, every year ahead.
Both work in the same space, the first principal components of the
effective cores (Step 1 below), and both start from the population's last
observed schedule.  They differ in how they move through that space: MDMx
extrapolates each population's own recent trend, damped and pulled towards
the HMD-wide drift; the flow field moves every population along the paths
that populations at the same level of mortality followed in the past.

::: {.callout-tip}
## What "e₀" means in this chapter and the next
Wherever an unqualified e₀ is quoted (an error, a bias, a coverage), it is
the **average of the female and the male e₀**, $\tfrac12(e_0^F + e_0^M)$, not
the e₀ of a combined-sex life table.
:::

## The MDMx forecaster, step by step

### Step 1 — the space, re-estimated at every origin

Every observed schedule is represented by its effective core
$\G_{c,t} = \bS^\top \M_{c,t}\, \bA$ (chapter 04), an $r_1 \times r_2$
matrix with `{python} r1` × `{python} r2` = `{python} r1 * r2` entries.
Forecasting `{python} r1 * r2` numbers jointly is wasteful, because they
move together: a principal-component analysis (PCA) of the vectorised
cores of all observed country-years (paper §9.1, Eq. 24),

$$
\mathbf{s}_{c,t} = \mathbf{V}^\top\big(\operatorname{vec}(\G_{c,t}) - \bar{\mathbf{g}}\big)
\in \R^{N_{PC}},
$$

with $N_{PC}$ = `{python} SET.n_pc` components, reduces each core to a
handful of *scores*, and the map back to a
schedule is linear (paper Eq. 25):
$\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{mean}} + \mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{s}$, where
column $j$ of $\mathbf{L}$ is the female and male $\logit(q_x)$ schedule
$\bS\,\mathbf{V}_j\,\bA^\top$ of the $j$-th loading.

A forecast made at origin $o$ must not use any data after $o$.  The bases
$\bS$ and $\bA$, the smoothing of $\bA$, the effective cores and the PCA
are therefore all **re-estimated at each origin from the observed
country-years up to that origin** (`mdmx.forecast.origin_space`), exactly
as chapter 04 estimates them from all the data.  The age rank is held at
the full-data value, $r_2$ = `{python} r2`, at every origin, so that the
forecasts from different origins have the same structure.  For the
latest year in the tensor this reproduces the decomposition of chapter 04
exactly, which the next cell checks.

### Step 2 — the drift target

Each population's own recent trend is informative, but noisy; the average
trend of all HMD populations is stable, but ignores what is particular to
the population.  MDMx blends the two (paper §9.2.1):

$$
\boldsymbol\delta_{\text{hier}} = w_{\text{HMD}}\,\boldsymbol\delta_{\text{HMD}}
 + w_{\text{country}}\,\boldsymbol\delta_{c},
$$

with weights $w_{\text{HMD}}$ = `{python} SET.weight_hmd` and
$w_{\text{country}}$ = `{python} SET.weight_country`.
A population's drift $\boldsymbol\delta_c$ is the average change of its
scores per **calendar year** over the window $[o-W, o]$, with
$W$ = `{python} SET.drift_window` years, measured between its first and last
observed years in the window (`mdmx.forecast.country_drifts`); missing and
exceptional years inside the window are simply not used, and the elapsed
time is counted in calendar years.  A population needs at least
`{python} SET.drift_min_years` observed years in the window for its drift
to count.  $\boldsymbol\delta_{\text{HMD}}$ is the average of the drifts of
all populations with a defined drift at that origin.  The target has no regime
level (paper §9.2.1), so this chapter does not depend on the clustering of
chapter 06.

### Step 3 — the drift-constrained Kalman filter

For each PCA score the model is a damped local linear trend whose drift
reverts not to zero but to the target (paper §9.2.2, Eqs. 26–27):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + \delta_{t-1} + \eta^{\ell}_t, &\operatorname{var}\eta^\ell &= q^\ell,\\
\delta_t &= \rho\,\delta_{t-1} + (1-\rho)\,\delta_{\text{hier}} + \eta^{\delta}_t,
  &\operatorname{var}\eta^\delta &= q^\delta,\\
y_t &= \ell_t + \varepsilon_t, &\operatorname{var}\varepsilon &= r,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $y_t$ is the observed score in calendar year $t$.  With $\rho<1$ the
forecast drift converges geometrically to the target, so a long-range
forecast follows the blended trend whatever the population did in its
last few years.  The covariances are diagonal, so the
`{python} n` components are independent two-state filters that share
$\rho$; `mdmx.forecast.llt_filter` writes this out and is checked against
the textbook matrix filter in `tests/test_forecast.py`.

The filter runs over **every calendar year** from the population's first
observed year to the origin.  In a year without an observed schedule —
a war or epidemic year set aside as exceptional, or a year missing from
the HMD series — the update step is skipped: the state is carried forward by
the model and its uncertainty grows, exactly as in a forecast.  The
$3N_{PC}+1$ = `{python} 3 * n + 1` parameters ($q^\ell$, $q^\delta$, $r$ for
each component, and the common $\rho$, kept between
`{python} SET.rho_bounds[0]` and `{python} SET.rho_bounds[1]`) maximise the Gaussian likelihood of the
observed years (prediction-error decomposition) by L-BFGS-B.  Many of
these maxima lie on a bound — a variance at its floor, which is effectively
zero, or $\rho$ at its lower bound — so the optimiser works on the standard
deviations and on $\rho$ itself, where such a maximum can be reached.  It
runs until $-\log L$ stops decreasing at machine precision, and a fit
counts as converged if the projected gradient there is below
`{python} f"{SET.mle_pg_flag:g}"`.
The likelihood can have more than one local maximum — typically one with
$\rho$ at its lower bound and a higher one inside the range — so the
optimiser is started from several points and the best fit is kept:
moment-based starting values ($q^\ell = \operatorname{var}\Delta y$,
$q^\delta = \operatorname{var}\Delta^2 y/4$, $r = \operatorname{var}\Delta y/10$,
within their bounds) with $\rho$ in the middle of its range and at
`{python} len(SET.mle_rho_starts)` fixed levels across
it, and the `{python} SET.mle_grid_top` best points of the *profile*
likelihood of $\rho$ on a grid of `{python} len(SET.mle_grid_rho)` values.
Given $\rho$, the components are independent, so the profile is cheap: the
standard deviations are found component by component by a grid search and
then fitted exactly at every grid value of $\rho$ at once.  Several starts
make a missed maximum unlikely, not impossible; the design was chosen by comparing
search strategies.  Chapter 12 reports convergence and the fitted $\rho$ by
origin for every fit of the evaluation, under both fitting periods.  How
often a single start would have missed the maximum is counted
[below](#sec-c11-starts) for the fits of the long-run forecasts, one per
population at the latest data year.

### Step 4 — from scores to schedules and e₀

A forecast of the scores is a forecast of the complete female and male
schedules, $\hat{\mathbf z}_{o+h} = \mathbf z_{\text{mean}} + \mathbf L\,
\hat{\mathbf s}_{o+h}$, and from those of e₀ for each sex.  The forecast
uncertainty of the scores at horizon $h$ is that of a future *observed*
score: the filter's state variance plus the observation noise $r$ (both
diagonal).  Because e₀ is a nonlinear function of the schedule, its
standard deviation follows from the delta method (paper §9.2):
$\sigma^2_{e_0}(h) = \sum_j J_j^2 \operatorname{var}(s_j)$ with
$J = \partial e_0/\partial \mathbf s = (\partial e_0/\partial\mathbf z)\,\mathbf L$.
The gradient $\partial e_0/\partial\mathbf z$ is computed exactly from the
life table (`mdmx.forecast.e0_and_gradient`): with
$e_0 = \sum_k w_k l_k$, $\partial e_0/\partial z_x = -q_x\sum_{k>x} w_k l_k$.
A Monte-Carlo check of the delta method is reported with the intervals.

### Step 5 — the jump-off anchor {#sec-fc-anchor}

The Kalman filter starts the forecast from the *filtered* level of the
scores, and the `{python} SET.n_pc` scores represent a schedule only up to
the PCA truncation.  The schedule the forecast starts from therefore differs
from the observed one, by a small amount at most ages but enough to move the
female − male gap of e₀ at the jump-off.  MDMx keeps the part of the observed schedule that
its scores do not represent (paper §9.2.4, Eq. 28): the residual of the observed schedule at the
job's last training year from its projection on the scores,
$\varepsilon_{c} = \mathrm{logit}\,q_{c,t_0} - (\bar z + L\,s_{c,t_0})$, is
added to every forecast schedule (`forecast.mdmx_origin(anchor="persistent")`,
the persistent anchor, ρ = 1).  The flow field uses
the same anchor.  A decaying anchor, with a half-life of
`{python} f"{float(E['decaying_anchor_half_life']):g}"` years, is a sensitivity in chapter 12.

The residual holds two things: the population's own detail, which the anchor
is there to keep, and the noise of that one year's single-age rates, which it
is not.  Carried as observed, the noise would stay in every forecast schedule
at every horizon.  The residual is therefore smoothed over age before it is
added, each sex's by a Whittaker smoother with second differences,
$\tilde\varepsilon = \arg\min_{u} \sum_x (u_x - \varepsilon_x)^2 + \nu \sum_x (\Delta^2 u_x)^2$
over ages 1 and over, with ν = `{python} f"{SET.anchor_smoothing:,.0f}"`; age 0 keeps
its observed value, because the step from infancy to childhood is real
(`forecast.smooth_residual`).  Both forecasters carry $\tilde\varepsilon_c$.
The smoothing was chosen on the evaluation's origins up to 2000, among
smoothers over age and over years, of the sex difference and of each sex;
chapter 12 shows what it does to the comparison's measures.

At every origin of the evaluation, the cell measures what the anchor keeps:
the e₀ and the gap of the projection plus the smoothed residual minus those of
the projection, and the share of the residual that the smoothing removes.

## The flow field

The flow field (paper §9.3) forecasts in MDMx's space (Step 1), from data up to the
origin only (`mdmx.flowfield`).  Its state is the vector of PCA scores of a
population-year, $s = (s_1, \dots, s_5)$; the first score is the level of
mortality, oriented so that a higher $s_1$ is lower mortality.

1. **The speed** (`flowfield.speed_function`).  Each population's $s_1$ is
   smoothed over its observed years, and its annual change is computed.  The
   speed $g(s_1)$ is a LOWESS of these changes against $s_1$, pooled over the
   populations: how fast populations at each level of mortality improved.
   It is never allowed to reverse the improvement.
2. **The frontier point** (`flowfield.orientation`).  $s_1^*$ is the median
   $s_1$ of the population-years whose e₀ is in the top
   `{python} f"{1 - FFS.frontier_quantile:.0%}"`.  Below it a
   forecast moves at the pace that populations at its level moved in the
   past.  Beyond it there is no past to learn from.
3. **The pace beyond the frontier** (`flowfield.frontier_level`,
   `frontier_speed`, `frontier_rule`).  In each year the `{python} FFS.frontier_k`
   populations with the highest e₀ lead; their mean $s_1$, chain-linked so that
   populations entering the data do not move it, gives the frontier's pace
   $V_s$ over the `{python} FFS.frontier_window` years before the origin.  Beyond
   $s_1^*$ the speed is $v_1 = \max\{0,\, w V_s + (1 - w)\, g(s_1^*)\}$, with
   $w = n/(n + n_0)$, $n_0$ = `{python} f"{FFS.frontier_n0:g}"`, for the $n$
   populations that led in those years.
4. **The shape law** (`flowfield.shape_law`).  The other scores follow
   $s_1$ along $f_k(s_1)$, a LOWESS of $s_k$ on $s_1$ over the last
   `{python} FFS.shape_window` years
   (along its tangent beyond the frontier point of those years), and a
   population's own deviation from $f_k$ decays at the pooled rate $\alpha_k$
   a year.
5. **The path** (`flowfield.integrate`, `forecast_origin`).  From the last
   observed scores, $s_1$ moves one year at a time at the speed of its level,
   the other scores follow the shape law, and the schedules carry the
   jump-off anchor with its smoothed residual (Step 5).

leaders' pace beyond the frontier) was chosen in development studies on the
development origins; chapter 12 describes how (the article's §10.9 says on which origins the form was selected).  The figure shows the flow fitted to every year up to the latest data.

### What the fitted flow says about mortality change

The flow field's fitted objects can be read as statements about how mortality
changes.  Read together, the speed and the shape law trace one path through
the space of schedules, the *typical path*: the level $s_1$ moves at the speed
$g(s_1)$ and the other scores sit on the shape law.  One year along it the
level moves to $s_1 + g(s_1)$, so the gain in e₀ that the flow implies at the
level $s_1$ is $e(s_1 + g(s_1)) - e(s_1)$, where $e(s_1)$ is the e₀ of the
typical schedule at that level (paper §11.4, Eq. 37).  Plotted against e₀,
this implied gain is the flow field's phase portrait of life expectancy: the
counterpart of bayesLife's gain function, estimated over the whole schedule
and without a parametric form.  The typical path also gives each sex's e₀, so
the gap between the sexes along the level of mortality.

The section describes the whole transition, so it uses the flow at the latest
data year with the shape law estimated on every year (`config/params.d/theory.toml`).
The forecaster's shape law uses the last 50 years only, when few populations
were at the lower levels.  Observed gains are centred over 10 years.

## Sex differences in the long run

MDMx forecasts both sexes at every age from one set of scores, so the age
pattern of the sex difference is forecast jointly — not held fixed by
assumption, as in the coherent FDM (whose sex ratios are stationary), nor
left free, as in Lee–Carter and Hyndman–Ullah.  How does it behave far
ahead?  The forecast drift of the scores reverts to the hierarchical
target δ_hier (Step 3), so in the long run the male − female difference of
logit(q_x) at age x changes at the constant rate
$(L_{\text{male}} - L_{\text{female}})\,\delta_{\text{hier}}$
(`mdmx.compare.sex_difference_slope`).  On the logit scale any non-zero
rate means that the difference keeps changing, so the question is how
fast.  The cell computes the rate for every population at the latest data
year, `{python} int(years[-1])` — split into the part that comes from the
HMD-wide drift (common to the populations) and the population's own
part — and compares the change it implies over 100 years with the change
actually observed over each population's last 50 years (as pre-specified).

## Summary

MDMx and the flow field forecast the same object, the complete schedule of
both sexes, in the same space, and both start from the observed schedule,
its departure from the scores smoothed over age.
MDMx extrapolates each population's own damped trend, pulled towards the
HMD-wide drift; the flow field moves each population along the paths that
populations at its level of mortality followed, at the leaders' pace beyond
the frontier.  Chapter 12 evaluates both, with the established methods,
from every origin between 1900 and 2015.
